Electromagnetism: induction & Maxwell

~30 min4 widgetsthe chapter where the wave appears

Chapters 1 and 2: currents make magnetic fields. This chapter is the other direction, and the punchline. A changing magnetic field makes an electric field. Put the two directions together and you get a field that can sustain itself in empty space, travelling at the speed of light.

Faraday: a changing B makes an E

In 1831 Michael Faraday pushed a magnet into a coil and saw a galvanometer needle jump. Hold the magnet still and nothing. Pull it out and the needle jumps the other way. The rule:

$$\mathcal E=-N\frac{d\Phi_B}{dt},\qquad \Phi_B=\int\vec B\cdot d\vec A\quad(\text{webers})$$

$\Phi_B$ is the magnetic flux, the amount of $\vec B$ passing through the loop. $\mathcal E$ is an electromotive force, a voltage you can measure. $N$ is the number of turns, since each turn adds its own. The minus sign is Lenz’s law: the induced current makes a field that opposes the change in flux. (If it didn’t, you’d get something for nothing: a perpetual motion machine.)

Everything that makes electricity from motion is this equation. A power-station generator spins coils in a magnetic field; a transformer uses one coil’s changing field to drive a second; a wireless charger and an induction cooktop do the same through the air; and a loop antenna receives a radio wave because the wave’s magnetic field changes through the loop.

A transformer in one line

Two coils share the same changing flux. Each turn sees the same $d\Phi/dt$, so the voltages are in the ratio of turns:

$$\frac{V_s}{V_p}=\frac{N_s}{N_p},\qquad V_pI_p\approx V_sI_s$$

Step up the voltage and the current falls: that’s why the national grid uses 400 kV on long lines, where $I^2R$ losses would otherwise be enormous. It only works with changing flux, which is why transformers need AC and a DC battery produces a single click.

Maxwell’s missing piece

By 1860 the laws looked like this. Charges make $\vec E$ (Gauss). There are no magnetic charges (Gauss for B). Changing $\vec B$ makes $\vec E$ (Faraday). Currents make $\vec B$ (Ampère). One thing bothered James Clerk Maxwell: Ampère’s law failed for a charging capacitor. Current flows into one plate and out of the other, but no current crosses the gap, so Ampère’s law gave a different answer depending on whether you drew your loop through the wire or through the gap.

His fix, in 1865: a changing electric field behaves like a current (he called it the displacement current) and makes a magnetic field. With that term, the four laws become consistent. Click through them:

The wave appears

Now stare at the last two cards together. A changing $\vec B$ makes a circulating $\vec E$. A changing $\vec E$ makes a circulating $\vec B$. Suppose you wiggle a field in one place. It makes the next patch of space change, which makes the one after that change, and so on: a disturbance that carries on by itself, with no wire and no medium. Work through the algebra (below) and the wave equation falls out:

$$\nabla^2\vec E=\mu_0\varepsilon_0\frac{\partial^2\vec E}{\partial t^2}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad c=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}}=2.998\times10^8\ \text{m/s}$$

Maxwell worked this number out from two table-top constants and found it matched the measured speed of light. He concluded, correctly, that light is an electromagnetic wave. Radio, microwaves, X-rays are the same thing at different frequencies; chapter 5 lines them all up.

Maths: from the two curl equations to the wave equation

In empty space with no charges or currents, Faraday and Ampère–Maxwell read $\nabla\times\vec E=-\partial_t\vec B$ and $\nabla\times\vec B=\mu_0\varepsilon_0\partial_t\vec E$. Take the curl of the first:

$$\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E)=-\partial_t(\nabla\times\vec B)=-\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t^2\vec E$$

The vector identity $\nabla\times(\nabla\times\vec E)=\nabla(\nabla\cdot\vec E)-\nabla^2\vec E$ with $\nabla\cdot\vec E=0$ leaves $-\nabla^2\vec E=-\mu_0\varepsilon_0\,\partial_t^2\vec E$. That is a wave equation with speed $1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$. The same steps for $\vec B$ give the same equation.

Anatomy of the wave

Take the simplest case, a plane wave travelling along $x$ with $\vec E$ pointing along $z$. Then $\vec H$ points along $y$, and:

  • $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ are in phase (they peak together) and perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel: it is a transverse wave.
  • Their sizes are tied: $E=\eta_0H$ with $\eta_0=377\ \Omega$.
  • The direction of travel is that of $\vec E\times\vec H$, the Poynting vector, which also gives the power per square metre: $S=\vec E\times\vec H$, so $S=E^2/\eta_0$ (peak) or $E_{rms}^2/377$.
  • Frequency and wavelength are tied by $c=f\lambda$.
RememberWave energy is split equally between electric and magnetic fields and flows in the direction E × H. An antenna’s whole job is to start such a wave off (transmit) or to catch the $\vec E$ or $\vec H$ of a passing one (receive).

What you now know

  • Changing magnetic flux through a loop induces a voltage $\mathcal E=-N\,d\Phi/dt$; Lenz’s minus sign means it opposes the change.
  • Maxwell added the displacement current: a changing $\vec E$ makes a $\vec B$ too.
  • Four equations: Gauss (E), Gauss for B, Faraday, Ampère–Maxwell.
  • They combine into a wave equation with speed $c=1/\sqrt{\mu_0\varepsilon_0}$.
  • In the wave $\vec E\perp\vec H\perp$ direction of travel, $E/H=377\ \Omega$, power flows along $\vec E\times\vec H$.
