Chapter 1 · Fields from scratch

A current makes a field

~20 min3 widgetsone or two formulas, explained

Put a battery across a wire and something invisible appears around it: a magnetic field. That one fact is the root of motors, transformers, radio and radar. Let’s build it from the ground up.

Charge and current

Matter is full of electric charge, measured in coulombs (C). One electron carries $-1.6\times10^{-19}$ C. A current is charge in motion: $I = \Delta Q/\Delta t$, so one ampere is one coulomb per second, about $6\times10^{18}$ electrons passing a point every second.

The electrons in a 10 A household wire crawl at well under a millimetre per second. What travels fast is the push: the electric field that sets them all moving at once, near the speed of light. A garden hose works the same way. Turn the tap and water comes out the far end immediately, though each water molecule moves slowly.

The field around a wire

In 1820 Hans Christian Ørsted noticed that a compass needle twitched when he switched a current on nearby. A moving charge, not just a magnet, makes a magnetic field $\vec B$. For a long straight wire the field circles the wire and gets weaker with distance:

$$B=\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi r},\qquad \mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \mathrm{T\,m/A}$$

$B$ is measured in tesla (T). $\mu_0$ is the permeability of free space, the constant that says how easily a vacuum lets a magnetic field form. The direction follows the right-hand rule: thumb along the current, fingers curl the way $\vec B$ circulates.

Try 10 A at 1 cm: $B=2\times10^{-7}\cdot10/0.01 = 2\times10^{-4}$ T, 200 µT, four times Earth’s field. This is why a compass wobbles near a charger cable.

Maths: where $B=\mu_0 I/2\pi r$ comes from (Ampère’s law)

Pick any closed loop and add up $\vec B\cdot d\vec l$ all the way round. The answer depends only on the current threading the loop:

$$\oint \vec B\cdot d\vec l=\mu_0 I_{\text{enclosed}}$$

For a straight wire, symmetry says $B$ has the same size all round a circle of radius $r$ and points along it. The loop is $2\pi r$ long, so $B\cdot2\pi r=\mu_0 I$. Done. For a messier wire you add up small pieces with the Biot–Savart law: a short piece $d\vec l$ carrying $I$ makes

$$d\vec B=\frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{I\,d\vec l\times\hat r}{r^2}$$

Note the $1/r^2$: a short piece of current falls off like a point source. A long wire is a pile of such pieces, and the pile falls off only like $1/r$. Chapter 4 explores this difference.

A loop is a tiny magnet

Bend the wire into a circle of radius $a$ and the field lines of all the pieces pass through the middle in the same direction. On the axis, at distance $z$ from the centre:

$$B_z=\frac{\mu_0 I a^2}{2\,(a^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\ \xrightarrow{\ z\gg a\ }\ \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{2\,m}{z^3},\qquad m=I\pi a^2$$

$m$ is the magnetic moment (A·m²). A bar magnet is, to a good approximation, the same thing: a lot of tiny atomic current loops lined up. Far away every such “dipole” falls off as $1/r^3$, much faster than a straight wire’s $1/r$. Remember that ladder: line 1/r, point 1/r², dipole 1/r³.

A steady current does not radiate

Here is the question that matters for the rest of the guide. A steady current makes a steady $\vec B$. That field stores energy, but nothing is sent away: the field around a DC wire just sits there, the same every second. No energy leaves, so a battery connected to a closed circuit radiates nothing. A charge moving at constant speed is the same: its field simply comes along for the ride.

To make a wave we need the field to change, and changing means the charge must accelerate. Why? The field at a distant point can only learn about the charge’s motion after a delay of $r/c$. If the charge suddenly stops, points beyond a sphere of radius $ct$ still “see” a charge that is carrying on. The two regions disagree, and the field has to bend sideways across the boundary. That sideways kink is a transverse electric field moving outward at the speed of light: a pulse of radiation.

The rule to remember

No acceleration, no radiation. Steady current (constant velocity): fields stay attached to the charges. Changing current (acceleration): a wave leaves. The faster the charges wiggle, the more is sent.

For a slowly moving charge the radiated power is given by the Larmor formula:

$$P=\frac{q^2a^2}{6\pi\varepsilon_0c^3}$$

It depends on acceleration squared. A charge oscillating at frequency $f$ with amplitude $x_0$ has $a=(2\pi f)^2x_0$, so the power grows like $f^4$. That is the first hint of why antennas get good only when the frequency is high enough, and why 50 Hz mains wiring is a very poor transmitter.

What you now know

  • Moving charge (current) makes a magnetic field $\vec B$; the right-hand rule gives its direction.
  • Near a long wire $B=\mu_0I/2\pi r$ ($\propto1/r$); a short piece of wire or a loop at distance falls faster.
  • Electrons drift slowly; the electric push travels at about $c$.
  • Steady currents and constant velocities do not radiate. Acceleration does, and the radiated power grows with $a^2$.
