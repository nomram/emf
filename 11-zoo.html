The families, and why they look the way they do

1 · Omnidirectional wire antennas

Dipole, monopole, folded dipole, whip. Radiate equally in all horizontal directions but not up or down (a doughnut). Ideal for broadcasting and mobile communication, where the other end may be anywhere. Gain 2–5 dBi at most. Everything you can build from a piece of wire at one wavelength or less.

2 · Loops: the magnetic antenna

A small loop responds to the magnetic field of a wave (Faraday: chapter 3), so it is largely deaf to local electric noise, which is why direction-finders, AM radios (the ferrite rod) and RFID readers use them. A large loop (circumference ≈ λ) behaves like a directional antenna (about 3 dBi) whose maximum is perpendicular to the loop.

3 · Parasitic arrays: Yagi–Uda and log-periodic

Hidetsugu Yagi and Shintaro Uda (1926) noticed that a passive rod near a driven element is excited by it and re-radiates with a phase set by its length. A slightly longer rod (the reflector) lags in phase and pushes the radiation forward; a slightly shorter one (a director) leads and draws it forward. Add several directors and the beam sharpens to as much as 12–15 dBi. The price is bandwidth of a few percent. If you need many frequencies at once, the log-periodic dipole array (LPDA) is a chain of dipoles whose length and spacing grow by a constant ratio: at each frequency a “resonant band” of dipoles acts like a Yagi, and 10:1 bandwidth is possible at 7–9 dBi gain.

4 · Circular polarisation: the helix

A wire wound as a corkscrew one wavelength per turn radiates along its axis in “axial mode”, producing circular polarisation with gain growing with the number of turns, about 10.8 dBi + 10 log(N/4). It is the choice for satellite links (no polarisation alignment), amateur satellite tracking and deep-space telemetry.

5 · Planar antennas: patch, slot, PIFA

Etch a copper rectangle on a circuit board: you have a patch. It is resonant when its length is half a wavelength in the dielectric, so high-permittivity boards shrink it. Gain is 6–8 dBi, the beam is broad, and bandwidth is small (a few percent). Phones use a variant called the PIFA (planar inverted-F antenna), tucked into a few cm³ and often tuned electronically across bands. GPS and Wi-Fi antennas in your laptop are patches or printed inverted-F designs. A slot is the complement of a dipole: a narrow slit in a metal sheet, which radiates like a dipole with $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ swapped (Babinet’s principle); aircraft use flush slots.

6 · Aperture antennas: horn and dish

At microwave frequencies the natural antenna is a hole that the wave leaves through. A flared waveguide, the horn, is wideband and predictable ($G=\eta4\pi A/\lambda^2$), so it is used as a calibration standard and as the feed of a reflector. A parabolic dish collects the whole wave front over its aperture and focuses it on a small feed. Its beamwidth is about $70\lambda/D$ degrees and gain $\eta(\pi D/\lambda)^2$. Use the dish widget below to see the geometry and the effect of surface errors.

What the numbers sayA 60 cm satellite-TV dish at 12 GHz: $D/\lambda=24$, gain ≈ 36 dBi, beamwidth ≈ 3°. That is why dishes must be pointed accurately at a geostationary satellite. A 70 m deep-space dish at 8.4 GHz has 74 dBi and a beam only about 0.04° wide, a fourteenth of the Moon’s apparent diameter. Surface roughness matters once it approaches λ/16: for 8.4 GHz (λ = 3.6 cm) that is 2 mm over a 70 m dish.

7 · Arrays: many antennas working together

Identical elements fed with controlled phases form a beam whose direction can be changed electronically. That is the phased array of chapters 18–19: the same idea as a Yagi, but with every element driven. The last widget in the zoo selection lets you steer one.

Size always scales with wavelength

What you now know

  • Wire antennas: omnidirectional, low gain. Parasitic and log-periodic arrays: directional, 7–15 dBi. Helix: circular polarisation.
  • Planar antennas are thin and printable but narrow-band; aperture antennas (horn, dish) give high gain by being many wavelengths wide.
  • Beamwidth ≈ $70\lambda/D$°, gain ≈ $\eta(\pi D/\lambda)^2$: every doubling of diameter gives +6 dB and halves the beam.
  • Antenna sizes scale with λ: from 1900 km at 76 Hz to 0.7 µm for light.
