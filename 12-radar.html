Chapter 12 · Radar

Radar from zero

~40 min3 widgetspulses, range, resolution, the 1/R⁴ equation

Shout in a canyon and time the echo: that is radar. “RADAR” stands for RAdio Detection And Ranging. The principle is just as simple, but the numbers are extreme: the echo you must detect can be a hundred million billion times weaker than the pulse you sent.

Time of flight

A radio wave travels at $c=3\times10^8$ m/s. Send a short pulse, listen, and time how long until the echo returns. The wave went out and back, so

$$R=\frac{c\,t}{2}\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad t=\frac{2R}{c}=6.67\ \mu\text{s per km}\ (12.36\ \mu\text{s per nautical mile})$$

An aircraft 100 km away returns its echo after 667 µs. At that speed the aircraft hardly moves; it is as if the world is frozen between transmission and reception. The antenna also tells you the bearing (the direction it is pointing when the echo arrives) and often the elevation; with Doppler (chapter 13) the radial speed. History: the idea was shown by Christian Hülsmeyer (1904) with ships and by the Americans and the British in the 1920s–30s; the British Chain Home stations, using 20–30 MHz pulses and huge antenna towers, gave early warning in the Battle of Britain in 1940.

The block diagram

A pulse radar has: a transmitter (a high-power oscillator or amplifier, formerly a magnetron, now often solid-state), a duplexer (a fast switch so the same antenna transmits and receives without the transmitter frying the receiver), the antenna (chapter 11), a sensitive receiver, a signal processor (filters, Doppler, detection) and a display. A monostatic radar has transmitter and receiver together; a bistatic radar puts them apart; a passive radar uses someone else’s transmissions (FM, TV, cell towers).

The pulse: three numbers you must know

  • Pulse length $\tau$: in space the pulse is $c\tau$ long (a 1 µs pulse is 300 m). Two targets closer than half of that merge into one blip: the range resolution is $\Delta R=c\tau/2=c/2B$, where $B\approx1/\tau$ is the bandwidth.
  • Pulse repetition frequency PRF (and period PRI $=1/\text{PRF}$): after each pulse the radar waits for echoes before sending the next. An echo from beyond the unambiguous range $R_u=c/(2\,\text{PRF})$ arrives after the next pulse was sent and is mistaken for a closer target. A 1500 Hz PRF gives $R_u=100$ km.
  • Duty cycle $\tau\cdot\text{PRF}$: the fraction of time spent transmitting, typically 0.1–1 % for pulsed radars. The average power is $P_{avg}=P_{peak}\cdot$ duty cycle, and it is the average power (energy) that decides detection.

Also, the radar cannot hear echoes while it transmits, so targets closer than $c\tau/2$ (the “blind range”) are invisible. Play with all of this; make the second target farther than the unambiguous range and watch it jump to the wrong place:

The pulse-compression trickTo see far you need energy (a long pulse); to separate nearby targets you need a short pulse (large bandwidth). These conflict. Pulse compression breaks the conflict: transmit a long pulse whose frequency sweeps across a bandwidth $B$ (a chirp), then correlate the echo with a copy of it in the receiver: the output is a sharp peak of width $1/B$. You get the energy of a 100 µs pulse and the resolution of a 0.1 µs one. Almost every modern radar does this; chapter 13’s FMCW radar takes it to the extreme.

Angular resolution: the antenna is the limit

Where is the target in angle? The antenna beam has a width $\theta_{3dB}\approx70\lambda/D$ degrees (chapter 11): a target anywhere in the beam gives an echo. A 3° beam at 100 km is $100\times0.052=5.2$ km wide, so cross-range resolution is poor compared with the range resolution, which can be 15 m. That mismatch is why aircraft on a radar screen look like wide arcs, and why synthetic aperture radar (chapter 14) exists.

The radar equation: how far can it see?

Take the transmitted power density at the target, $S_t=P_tG/(4\pi R^2)$ (chapter 4). The target intercepts it and re-radiates some of it back; its “size” as a reflector is the radar cross section $\sigma$ in m² (chapter 15): the echo power density back at the radar is $S_t\sigma/(4\pi R^2)$. The antenna captures $A_e=G\lambda^2/4\pi$ of that:

$$P_r=\frac{P_tG^2\lambda^2\sigma}{(4\pi)^3R^4}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad R_{max}=\left[\frac{P_tG^2\lambda^2\sigma}{(4\pi)^3S_{min}L}\right]^{1/4}$$

The crucial thing is the fourth power of $R$: the signal spreads twice (out, then back). It means range grows only as the fourth root of power: to see twice as far you need 16× the transmitter power (or 4× the antenna gain, since $G$ appears squared). A bird (0.001 m²) and a jumbo jet (100 m²) differ by 10⁵ in σ, and in detection range by only $10^{5/4}=18$. The widget lets you try it with real values.

What limits $S_{min}$? Noise.

Every receiver has thermal noise power $N=kT_0BF$ with Boltzmann’s constant $k=1.38\times10^{-23}$, $T_0=290$ K, bandwidth $B$ and noise figure $F$. At 1 MHz bandwidth, $kT_0B=4\times10^{-15}$ W ($-114$ dBm); add a 4 dB noise figure and a required SNR of 13 dB for reliable detection and $S_{min}\approx-97$ dBm. That’s what the radar “hears” at its maximum range. A modest 100 kW transmitter and a good antenna can detect a 5 m² target at 100+ km, where the echo is $10^{-13}$ W: a ten-trillionth of a watt. Narrower bandwidth (longer pulses or more integration) lowers $N$, which is the same reason GPS and deep-space links work (chapter 9).

Detection: signal or noise?

The receiver output is a noisy voltage. A threshold separates “target” from “no target”. Set it too low and noise spikes cause false alarms; too high and weak targets are missed. Practical radars use constant false alarm rate (CFAR) processing, which measures the local noise level and sets the threshold a fixed amount above it, and integrate many pulses (each hit adds, noise only averages) to dig out weak signals: with $n$ pulses the SNR improves by up to $n$.

The radar screen

The classic display is the PPI, the plan-position indicator: a map with the radar at the centre, the sweep rotating with the antenna, and blips where echoes arrive. Try it: turn the beamwidth up (arcs get wider), the detection range down, and switch the clutter on. The conventional aircraft show far out; the stealth fighter, the drone and the bird flock are visible only up close.

The “clutter” is echoes from things you do not want: ground, sea, rain, birds, wind turbines. Chapter 13 shows how Doppler processing separates the moving from the stationary.

What you now know

  • Range $R=ct/2$; 6.67 µs per km of range.
  • Range resolution $c\tau/2=c/2B$; unambiguous range $c/2\text{PRF}$; blind range $c\tau/2$; duty cycle $\tau\text{PRF}$.
  • Pulse compression gives the energy of a long pulse with the resolution of a short one.
  • Angular resolution is the beamwidth $\approx70\lambda/D$°.
  • Radar equation: $P_r\propto P_tG^2\lambda^2\sigma/R^4$. Doubling the range costs 16× the power.
  • Detection is limited by thermal noise $kTBF$; thresholds, CFAR and integration set false alarms and sensitivity.
