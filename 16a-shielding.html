Shielding in practice: cages, waveguides & feedthroughs

~50 min8 widgetshow to build a box that keeps its promise

A Faraday cage is easy to draw and hard to build. The metal is the easy part. The hard part is everything that has to cross the metal: power, data, air, a door. This chapter is the engineering answer to “how do I connect a cable to something inside a shielded box without letting the outside in?”, including how you charge a device in a cage.

What a Faraday cage does (and the one-line proof)

Inside a closed hollow conductor, an external electric field is cancelled: the free charges in the metal move until their own field is equal and opposite to the outside field at every point inside. Michael Faraday showed it in 1836 by sitting in a wooden cube covered with metal foil while a huge electrical machine threw sparks at it, and feeling nothing. For a time-varying field the cancelling currents flow in a thin layer on the outer surface, set by the skin depth (chapter 6): the field decays as $e^{-t/\delta}$ through the wall. A closed metal shell therefore works as a shield against both static and radio fields, provided it has no holes and is thicker than a few skin depths (chapter 6’s table: 1 µm of copper stops 2.45 GHz).

Everyday examples: a car in a thunderstorm (the current flows over the body, not through you); a coaxial cable (the shield is a rolled-up cage for the centre wire); an aircraft hit by lightning; an MRI room (a cage keeps radio noise out of the scanner, and the scanner’s signals in); the metal case around a power supply; the ESD bag around a chip.

The principle to keep in mindA shield is one continuous conducting surface. Everything that crosses it is a hole, or an antenna, or both. The art of shielding is to make each crossing behave like a piece of the wall.

The limits of a Faraday cage

People often talk about cages as magic boxes. They are not. Here is where a real one falls short:

  1. Holes and seams leak, and the length of a gap matters, not its area. A slot of length $l$ leaks about $20\log_{10}(\lambda/2l)$ dB (chapter 16), nothing at all when $l\ge\lambda/2$. A 30 cm seam between two panels is a perfect slot antenna for 500 MHz. A ventilation grille, a screw pitch that is too wide, a door that does not make contact all the way round: all leak. This is why doors in shielded rooms use knife-edge and finger-stock gaskets that press metal on metal.
  2. Thin walls don’t stop low frequencies. Absorption $A=8.686\,t/\delta$ is tiny when $t\ll\delta$: a 0.5 mm aluminium sheet has $\delta=12$ mm at 50 Hz, so it stops almost nothing at mains frequency, while 85 µm skin depth at 1 MHz makes it superb. Low-frequency electric fields are still blocked (the charges rearrange), but low-frequency magnetic fields need either a very thick conductor or a high-permeability material (mu-metal, $\mu_r\approx10^4$–$10^5$) that conducts the field lines around the inside space, usually in layers. Even then, a static magnetic field (a magnet, or Earth’s field) is not shielded by any ordinary cage at all.
  3. The box has its own resonances. A metal cavity rings at $f_{mnp}=\tfrac c2\sqrt{(m/a)^2+(n/b)^2+(p/d)^2}$ (chapter 17). A 3 m room has a resonance near 70 MHz; a pulse or noise at that frequency may be amplified inside by the cavity’s Q. Shielded chambers line their walls with RF absorber (carbon-loaded foam, ferrite tiles) for exactly this reason.
  4. The cage works in both directions. It keeps radiation out, but also keeps the device’s own emissions in. A phone in a good bag is silent and deaf. A cage will not help a device that is already misbehaving or an antenna you wanted to hear.
  5. Penetrations decide everything (next sections). A cable, a pipe, a data line, a power cord: each crossing needs a deliberate design. A single unfiltered wire through a perfect box makes a box with a 60 dB hole.
  6. Grounding is not what makes it shield. At radio frequencies a floating box shields equally well. Grounding matters for safety (fault current, lightning), for static electricity and for very low-frequency electric fields. A badly grounded cage can however conduct noise currents to a place where you don’t want them: ground loops.
  7. Real-world numbers are modest. Typical shielding effectiveness (SE), measured in the 100 MHz–1 GHz range: aluminium-foil wrap with gaps: 20–40 dB; a steel ammo can with a rubber gasket: 30–50 dB; a commercial “Faraday bag” (conductive fabric, folded closed) 40–80 dB if sealed carefully, much less if the fold leaks; a welded-steel enclosure with fingers on the door: 80–100 dB; a purpose-built shielded room, copper or steel, filtered penetrations: 100–120 dB. (MIL-STD-285, IEEE 299 and IEC 61587-3 describe how to measure it.) A test anyone can do: put an AM/FM radio, or a phone you call, in the enclosure with the lid closed. If you can still hear it or ring it, the shield is poor.

For a whole box, the leaks add up: wall, holes, seams, and the box’s own resonance. The designer below keeps the budget: change the metal, the holes and the gasket and see which path dominates at each frequency.

Windows: shielding you can see through

Every cage has the same awkward requirement: eventually someone needs to look into it. A display, a lab-instrument window, an MRI-room window, an aircraft canopy, or a cabinet door with a screen: all need a transparent opening that stops radio. The answer is a conductor so thin or so sparse that light passes: transparent conducting oxides (ITO, FTO), silver nanowires, graphene, conductive polymers, ultra-thin silver, and metal meshes of wires too fine to see. Every one is a trade-off between sheet resistance (shielding) and transparency. Ordinary low-E energy-saving window glass is also a thin silver film, which is why modern windows attenuate your phone signal by 20–40 dB.

Waveguides: two very different jobs

A waveguide is a hollow metal tube. It has two roles, and understanding both gives you the master key for penetrations.

1 · The tube that carries a wave (above cutoff)

For microwaves, a hollow rectangular tube of width $a$ is a better transmission line than a coaxial cable: no centre conductor, no dielectric, very low loss, very high power handling. It supports a wave only above a cutoff frequency $f_c=c/2a$ (the TE$_{10}$ mode). The oven’s magnetron feeds its cavity through a WR-340 guide (86 × 43 mm, $f_c=1.74$ GHz) because 2.45 GHz is just above cutoff. Radar transmitters, satellite ground stations, and radio-astronomy front-ends all use waveguide for the same reason.

2 · The tube that blocks a wave (below cutoff)

Make the same tube narrow enough that the operating frequency is below its cutoff, and waves can’t propagate in it: the field falls off as $e^{-\alpha x}$, at about 32 dB per diameter for a round tube. A tube three diameters long therefore gives about 100 dB at every frequency below cutoff. That’s a hole that waves can’t use but fibres, air, water and mechanical shafts can. Such waveguide-below-cutoff tubes are the standard way to bring a fibre-optic cable, a coolant pipe, a pneumatic hose or a plastic shaft into a shielded room. Honeycomb vents are bundles of many short small tubes: air flows, waves don’t.

The one forbidden thingNever run a metal conductor through a below-cutoff tube. A metal wire or even a long conducting hose inside it is a coaxial line: it propagates all frequencies right through, and the tube’s “cutoff” no longer matters. Use glass fibre, plastic or ceramic in the tube. That is why optical fibres are the perfect wall-crossers: they cannot carry radio waves at all.

Getting a cable in without letting the field in

A conductor that passes through a shield carries noise along it, just like an antenna: the outside field induces currents along the outer part, and those currents flow on straight into the box. The fix is to arrange for the noise current to be caught at the wall and sent back outside, and not let it past.

Trick 1 · Let the shield’s skin effect do the work (shielded cables)

In a coaxial cable at high frequency, current on the outside of the shield and current on its inside are electrically separate: skin effect keeps each on its own surface. If the shield is clamped around its whole circumference to the wall at a bulkhead connector, the noise current on the outer surface flows into the wall and stays outside; only the wanted signal current on the inner surface continues inside. The shield becomes a part of the wall. If instead the shield is connected by a pigtail, a wire to a single point, the pigtail’s inductance (≈ 10 nH per cm) lets the outer-surface current leak to the inside: the shielding collapses at tens of MHz. Trick 2 · Filter every wire at the wall

A wire carrying power or slow signals can be given a low-pass filter at the point of entry, mounted through the wall so that the dirty and clean sides are separated by the metal. A series inductor (or common-mode choke) and a shunt capacitor to the wall make 40 dB per decade above the corner frequency. But real parts have parasitics: the capacitor’s leads and the inductor’s windings make the filter stop working at high frequency. Trick 3 · Don’t use a conductor at all

  • Optical fibre for data, through a below-cutoff tube, gives unlimited isolation, no ground loops, and gigabits. Media converters at the wall (copper ↔ fibre) are the standard interface to equipment inside shielded rooms. (Chapter 15a: the same property is why fibre-controlled drones are immune to radio interference.)
  • Optocouplers and isolation transformers pass a signal or power with no galvanic connection: a stage of defence against common-mode noise.
  • Dielectric and mechanical couplings for everything else: plastic rods for switches, glass windows with fine mesh (the oven door), ceramic feed-throughs, fibre-optic pressure sensors.

Trick 4 · Entry panel: one place, one design

Shielded rooms put every crossing on a single penetration panel, a bonded metal plate with filtered power, data connectors, fibre ports and a waveguide-below-cutoff array. Rules of thumb: one entry point (otherwise noise finds the loophole); the filters and bulkhead connectors in the wall, not on cables inside; the dirty (outside) wiring physically separated from the clean (inside) wiring; keep cable lengths on the clean side short; ferrite chokes on any cable that still has to run inside; and test the finished room by walking around it with a field probe while a transmitter radiates outside.

So, how do you charge a device in a Faraday cage?

The question has a surprisingly rich answer, because a charger means energy crossing the wall, and energy carried by a conductor brings noise with it. Your options, from simplest to most exotic:

  1. Bring a battery. Charge it outside; seal it in. Nothing crosses the wall. For the small, rarely-used device it is the best solution: 100 Wh is a 500 g Li-ion pack.
  2. A filtered mains or DC feedthrough. The usual answer for anything above a few watts: a power-line filter bolted through the wall, mains on the outside, filtered mains inside, optionally with a shielded isolation transformer (an electrostatic screen between primary and secondary) for isolation and safety. Typical SE 80–110 dB. Mind the leakage-current rule: the capacitor from line to earth is limited to a few nF, so most of the high-frequency rejection comes from chokes.
  3. Power over fibre. A laser outside sends light down a fibre; a photovoltaic converter inside produces up to a few watts of electricity at roughly 20–30 % overall efficiency. The wall is untouched. Used where isolation is critical (high-voltage equipment, MRI, explosive atmospheres).
  4. Through-wall ultrasonic power. Piezoelectric transducers on each side of an intact metal wall couple vibration through it. Tens of watts have been shown; the wall is not breached.
  5. Inductive (wireless) charging. Only works if the magnetic field gets through the wall, which, by definition, means the wall is not shielding at the charger’s frequency. A phone-charging coil at 100 kHz needs a non-conducting window; a metal cage blocks it. (Some enclosures deliberately do shield only above a few MHz, so low-frequency inductive charging passes through: a frequency-selective design, not a general Faraday cage.)
Reality check for “Faraday bags”

A bag with a charging cable out of one end is not a shield: the cable leaks. A bag that is folded but not sealed leaks along the fold. A phone that ‘still has signal’ proves the bag is imperfect; a phone that ‘loses signal’ proves only a few tens of dB at that frequency, not 100. Nothing is protected against a pulse unless the penetration problem is solved, as above.

Design checklist

  1. Decide the frequencies you need to stop and the required dB. Low-frequency magnetic fields need thick or high-μ metal; microwaves need small holes.
  2. Make the shell continuous (welded, soldered, or overlapped with conductive gaskets at ≤ λ/20 pitch) with no long slots; doors with finger stock or knife-edge.
  3. Replace every opening with a mesh or honeycomb of tubes below cutoff (depth ≥ 3 × width).
  4. Put all entries on one panel; use 360° shield bonding, filtered connectors, feedthrough capacitors, common-mode chokes and fibre for data.
  5. Power the device from a battery, filtered line, isolation transformer or power-over-fibre; never a bare cord.
  6. Line the inside with absorber if cavity resonances matter.
  7. Measure the finished enclosure; assume the weakest crossing sets the result.

What you now know

  • A Faraday cage works by surface currents cancelling the field inside; it needs thickness ≫ skin depth, a continuous surface, and no antennas through it.
  • Limits: slot length, seams and doors, thin walls at low frequency, magnetic fields (mu-metal), static magnetic fields (none), cavity resonances, penetrations.
  • A tube below cutoff blocks waves (≈ 32 dB per diameter for a round tube) but lets fibres, air and plastic through; above cutoff, a tube is a waveguide.
  • Coax shields bonded 360° at the wall use the skin effect to keep outer noise currents out; a pigtail ruins it above tens of MHz.
  • Filters work only until their parasitic resonances; feedthrough capacitors push that to GHz.
  • Powering a device in a cage: battery, filtered line (+ isolation transformer), power-over-fibre, ultrasonic through the wall; inductive charging breaks the shield by definition.
