What passes through what

~35 min2 big widgetsglass, plastic, concrete, soil, rock, seawater, you

Your phone works inside a wooden house, struggles inside a concrete one, and fails in a basement. A submarine can hear a message at 30 m depth but not at 3 m on the VHF radio. Light goes through your window but not your wall. Same family of waves, wildly different fates. This chapter explains why, with numbers.

What a wave can do to a material

When a wave meets a material one of three things happens, and they add up to 100 % of the power:

  1. Reflected: it bounces off the surface. Strong when the material’s “wave impedance” differs a lot from air’s 377 Ω: metals and sea water reflect nearly everything.
  2. Absorbed: the wave’s field pushes charges and rotates molecules, and the energy ends up as heat. This is what happens inside a lossy material and decides how deep the wave gets.
  3. Transmitted: it comes out the other side, weaker.

The material is described by two numbers, from chapter 2: the permittivity $\varepsilon_r$ (how much it stores electric field, which slows the wave) and the conductivity $\sigma$ (how freely charges move, which absorbs the wave). A convenient single measure is the loss tangent $\tan\delta=\sigma/\omega\varepsilon$ (how conductive relative to how much it stores):

  • $\tan\delta\ll1$: a good insulator (dielectric). The wave passes with little loss (dry glass, plastic, ice, dry air).
  • $\tan\delta\gg1$: a good conductor. The wave is stopped within a short distance, and mostly reflected (metals, seawater at low frequency).

A material can be both, depending on frequency, because $\omega$ is in the denominator. Seawater has $\tan\delta=\sigma/\omega\varepsilon\approx 4/(2\pi f\cdot80\cdot8.85\times10^{-12})$: at 100 Hz it is about $10^{7}$ (a conductor), at 1 GHz it is about 1, and at 100 GHz it behaves like a lossy dielectric (water molecules rotating). So the same water is a mirror at ELF and a sponge at microwaves.

Skin depth: how deep a wave goes into a conductor

In a good conductor the wave field dies off exponentially: $E(x)=E_0e^{-x/\delta}$. The distance $\delta$ at which the field falls to $1/e$ (37 %) is the skin depth:

$$\delta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}}\approx\frac{503}{\sqrt{f\,\mu_r\,\sigma}}\ \text{m}\qquad(f\text{ in Hz},\ \sigma\text{ in S/m})$$

Two lessons. Higher frequency, shallower: $\delta\propto1/\sqrt f$. And good conductors have micrometre skin depths at microwave frequencies, which is why a thin foil makes a perfect shield for them: the microwave oven needs only a thin metal box. (For 50 Hz magnetic fields you need ≈ cm of metal, which is why transformer shields are thick steel.)

The big picture: which band goes through which material

Patterns worth noticing:

  • Low frequencies go through nearly everything non-metallic. The longer the wavelength, the less the material can “see” the details, and the lower the conductive loss. That is why AM radio works indoors, why mines use through-earth radio, why submarines use ELF.
  • The microwave range is a sweet spot (Wi-Fi, cell phones, TV): it passes through walls and glass well enough, but antennas are small.
  • Millimetre waves and above are line-of-sight only: even a wall or your hand blocks 5G mmWave.
  • Light and infrared are blocked by almost everything solid except glass, plastics and clean water. But light is the only thing (besides low frequencies) that goes well through water.
  • X-rays and gamma rays come back because their photon energy is so high that atoms barely notice them; density and atomic number decide (lead, concrete, bone vs flesh).

Reading decibels3 dB: half the power. 10 dB: a tenth. 20 dB: 1 %. 30 dB: 0.1 %. Wi-Fi works with about 90–100 dB of total path loss, so a 15 dB wall costs you a lot but not everything. A signal with 100 dB of extra loss is gone, whatever the transmitter.

Holes and Faraday cages

A metal shield does not need to be solid. A mesh with holes much smaller than the wavelength acts as a solid wall, because the field cannot “squeeze through”. This explains the mesh in the window of a microwave oven: holes of ~2 mm are 60× smaller than the 12 cm wavelength, but 4000 times bigger than a light wavelength, so you can see through them but the microwaves cannot get out. Chapter 16 puts numbers on it with a shielding-effectiveness calculator, and chapter 17 uses it to look inside the oven.

Another surprise: mirrorsA metal mirror reflects light for the same reason it reflects radio: the free electrons shuffle in response and re-radiate a wave that cancels the incoming one inside the metal. The “skin” is just that re-radiation. A satellite dish and your bathroom mirror are the same device on different scales.

What you now know

  • A wave meeting matter is reflected, absorbed or transmitted. Insulators (low $\tan\delta$) transmit; conductors reflect and absorb in a skin depth $\delta=1/\sqrt{\pi f\mu\sigma}$.
  • Lower frequency generally penetrates farther; ELF/VLF are the only radio waves that go far into seawater or soil.
  • Glass, plastic and dry wood are nearly transparent to radio; concrete costs 10–40 dB at GHz frequencies; water kills microwaves; metal blocks almost everything.
  • Visible light is an exception: it passes through glass and clean water; X-rays and gamma pass because the photon energy is high.
