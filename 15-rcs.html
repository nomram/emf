Seen or unseen: RCS & stealth

~35 min2 widgetswhy shape and wavelength matter more than size

The radar equation has one factor that belongs to the target: $\sigma$, the radar cross section. It is not the target’s size. A large smooth sphere can be less visible than a small corner of metal. This chapter explains what $\sigma$ measures, and the physics (not the engineering recipes) behind “low observable” design.

What is radar cross section?

Imagine the target as an antenna that is “illuminated” by the radar’s wave and re-radiates some of it. $\sigma$ is the area of a perfect isotropic scatterer that would return the same power to the radar as the real target:

$$\sigma=4\pi R^2\,\frac{S_{\text{reflected at radar}}}{S_{\text{incident at target}}}\qquad[\text{m}^2]\ \text{or}\ 10\log_{10}\sigma\ [\text{dBsm}]$$

It depends on the target’s material, its size in wavelengths, its shape, the viewing angle and the polarisation. Typical values (for microwave radars):

(Published values for combat aircraft are classified or speculative; those quoted in the media are rough.) Because of the $1/R^4$ law, detection range depends on $\sigma^{1/4}$: reduce $\sigma$ by a factor 10 and the range falls by only $10^{-1/4}=0.56$; by 1000, to 0.18.

Size relative to wavelength: three regions

A target’s RCS depends dramatically on whether it is smaller or bigger than the wavelength. The cleanest example is a perfectly conducting sphere, for which the exact answer is known (the Mie series, which the widget computes):

  • Rayleigh region ($r\ll\lambda$): $\sigma\approx9\pi r^2(2\pi r/\lambda)^4\propto\lambda^{-4}$. Halve the wavelength and the echo is 16 times larger. Raindrops (mm) are Rayleigh scatterers at 3–10 cm: the reflectivity of rain goes as drop diameter to the sixth power, summed per unit volume: the weather-radar $Z$.
  • Resonance region ($r\sim\lambda/2\pi$ to $10\lambda/2\pi$): strong, rippling dependence on size and frequency, up to 3.6 times the geometric area.
  • Optical region ($r\gg\lambda$): geometric optics works: $\sigma\to\pi r^2$ for a sphere. Fortunately for design, this is the region where we can shape targets.

Shape: mirrors, corners and balls

In the optical region a smooth, flat or curved surface acts as a mirror for the radar wave. What matters is where the mirror reflections go:

  • A flat plate of area $A$ seen face-on gives $\sigma=4\pi A^2/\lambda^2$: for 1 m² at 10 GHz that is $1.4\times10^4$ m² (a mirror pointing back at the source). Tilt it by a few beamwidths of its own pattern (a few degrees!) and the echo drops by 30–40 dB: the lobe has width about $\lambda/a$.
  • A sphere returns $\pi r^2$ from every direction: no bright spot, but also never dark.
  • A trihedral corner returns the incoming ray back along its path, from a wide range of angles: $\sigma=4\pi a^4/3\lambda^2$. This is why corner reflectors are used as calibration targets and as boat reflectors, and why right-angled structures (ship decks meeting bulkheads, an aircraft wing joined to a fuselage, a car’s corners) are so visible.
  • A cylinder is bright broadside and dim end-on.

What “stealth” means physically

“Stealth” (low observability) does not mean invisible; it means smaller $\sigma$ in the directions and bands that matter, so that enemy radars detect the aircraft later, at shorter range, with less accuracy. The physics gives a short list of principles, which are all visible in the widget above:

  1. Shaping: avoid surfaces and right angles that face the radar. Tilt flat faces so that specular reflections go off in directions away from the source; align edges (wings, tail, doors) to a small set of directions, so the unavoidable spikes concentrate in a few narrow angles. Remove corner reflectors by blending joints and hiding or angling the weapon bays, inlets and engines.
  2. Radar-absorbent material (RAM) and coatings: layers of carefully tuned lossy or magnetic material convert part of the incident wave to heat or cancel the reflection by interference (a quarter-wave layer reflects with the opposite phase, like an anti-reflection coating on a lens). They work over a limited band.
  3. Edges, gaps and cavities: after the specular spike is dealt with, what remains is diffraction from edges and re-radiation from cavities, such as engine inlets and antenna openings, which must be shielded, bent or screened.
  4. Management of emissions: the aircraft’s own radar and radio transmissions are detectable far beyond its reflected echo. A low-probability-of-intercept design (chapter 15a) matters as much as a low RCS.
Why wavelength is the weak point

These tricks work when the features are larger than the wavelength. Long-wavelength radars (VHF and UHF, with λ of a metre or more, comparable to a wing or fuselage) see the whole airframe in the resonance region, where shaping and thin RAM coatings are much less effective. The same aircraft can look a thousand times larger to a VHF radar than to an X-band one. That is why low-frequency surveillance radars still matter. It is also why stealth is a reduction, not magic: it also doesn’t hide heat, visible light, sound, or the transmissions the platform makes, and it can be countered by multistatic radar (the reflection may be strong at a different angle), passive detection (using other sources) and by arrays that can integrate for a long time.

Beyond aircraft

  • Birds, insects and bats are routinely seen by weather and airport radars: nocturnal migration studies and bird-strike warning use them.
  • Drones have tiny RCS, plastic bodies and rotor micro-Doppler: detecting them is an active research area using small-wavelength radars and micro-Doppler classification.
  • Wind turbines are huge, rotating clutter sources that confuse air-traffic radars: the blades’ Doppler looks like aircraft.
  • Radar reflectors for safety: small boats and the masts of offshore wind farms carry corner reflectors so that ships’ radars see them.

What you now know

  • RCS $\sigma$ is the area of an isotropic scatterer giving the same echo; it depends on material, shape, angle, polarisation and size in wavelengths.
  • Rayleigh ($\sigma\propto\lambda^{-4}$), resonance and optical regions; a large sphere returns $\pi r^2$; a flat plate $4\pi A^2/\lambda^2$ face-on; a corner reflector $4\pi a^4/3\lambda^2$ over a wide angle.
  • Detection range $\propto\sigma^{1/4}$: a 1000× smaller RCS shortens range to 18 %.
  • Low-observable design rests on shaping, absorbing materials, edge and cavity control and emission control; it is weakest at long wavelengths.
