Chapter 10 · Antennas

Feeding it: lines & matching

~35 min2 widgetsreflections, VSWR, the Smith chart

The antenna is at the top of a mast; the radio is in the shed. Between them runs a cable, and at radio frequencies a cable is not just a wire. It is a waveguide for voltage and current waves with its own personality. Get the match between transmitter, cable and antenna wrong and part of your power bounces back and forth. This chapter shows why, and how to fix it.

Why a cable is more than a wire

For a 50 Hz mains wire a metre long, the voltage is the same everywhere along it, because the wavelength is 6000 km. At 100 MHz the wavelength is 3 m, so a cable 3 m long holds a full cycle of the wave inside it. The voltage at one end is different from the voltage at the other, and the cable behaves as a transmission line: waves travel along it, at a speed $v=c\cdot VF$ (the velocity factor, 0.66 for solid polyethylene coax) and with a characteristic impedance

$$Z_0=\sqrt{\frac{L'}{C'}}\qquad\Big(\text{for coax: }Z_0=\frac{138}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}}\log_{10}\frac{D}{d}\ \Omega\Big)$$

where $L'$ and $C'$ are the inductance and capacitance per metre. $Z_0$ is the ratio $V/I$ of a wave moving along the line. It is a property of the cable’s geometry, not of its length or the frequency, and is not something an ohmmeter reads. Common values: 50 Ω (radio, test equipment), 75 Ω (TV and video), 300 Ω (flat twin-lead), 600 Ω (open ladder line).

Mismatch means reflection

A wave on the line reaches the load and finds $Z_L$. If $Z_L=Z_0$ the wave looks like it continues on forever and all power goes into the load. If not, the load cannot absorb a wave with the ratio $V/I=Z_0$, so part of the wave is reflected back toward the generator:

$$\Gamma=\frac{Z_L-Z_0}{Z_L+Z_0},\qquad \text{VSWR}=\frac{1+|\Gamma|}{1-|\Gamma|},\qquad \text{RL}=-20\log_{10}|\Gamma|\ \text{dB}$$
  • $Z_L=Z_0$: $\Gamma=0$, VSWR 1:1. Perfect.
  • Short circuit ($Z_L=0$): $\Gamma=-1$, everything reflects, with inverted voltage.
  • Open circuit ($Z_L=\infty$): $\Gamma=+1$, everything reflects.
  • $Z_L=100\ \Omega$ on 50 Ω: $\Gamma=1/3$, VSWR 2:1, 11 % of the power reflected, return loss 9.5 dB.

The forward and reflected waves add up into a standing wave (a pattern frozen in space where the peaks and valleys sit still). VSWR (voltage standing-wave ratio) is the peak voltage divided by the valley voltage. Watch it on the line, with the matching Smith chart on its right.

The three-minute Smith chart

Every possible load $Z_L$ (every resistance and reactance) maps to one point inside the unit circle of the complex plane $\Gamma$. The Smith chart simply draws a grid of constant-resistance circles and constant-reactance arcs on top, so you can read impedance straight off. The centre is a perfect match. The left edge is a short; the right edge an open circuit. The top half is inductive; the bottom half is capacitive. Moving along the line toward the generator rotates the point clockwise around the centre by one full turn per half wavelength. That is all you need: a matching problem becomes “move this point to the centre”.

So a VSWR of 2:1 is by no means a catastrophe: half a decibel of loss. Two real dangers are different: (1) the reflected power adds to the cable’s own loss, since it travels the cable several times; (2) high standing-wave voltage can arc inside connectors or damage a transmitter’s output transistors, which is why many transmitters automatically reduce power at VSWR > 2–3.

Cable loss: frequency matters

Real cables are not lossless. The loss per length rises with $\sqrt f$ (skin effect in the conductors, chapter 6) and with $f$ (dielectric loss), so a cable that is fine at 10 MHz can eat most of your signal at 5 GHz:

And a mismatch costs more with a lossy cable, because the reflected power is attenuated twice.

Balanced vs unbalanced: the balun

A dipole is balanced: two arms with equal and opposite currents. Coax is unbalanced: signal on the inner wire, ground on the shield. Connect them directly and some current flows on the outside of the shield, turning the cable into part of the antenna; the pattern is distorted and you can pick up noise. A balun (balanced–unbalanced transformer, often a few turns of coax on a ferrite ring) fixes that. A 1:4 balun also converts a 300 Ω folded dipole to 75 Ω, or a 200 Ω load to 50 Ω.

How to match

If the antenna’s impedance is not $Z_0$, you have three classic tools:

  1. Change the antenna: bend it, fold it, add a ground plane, adjust its length. A half-wave dipole is 73 Ω; a folded dipole is 292 Ω; a dipole with arms drooped 120° apart is ≈ 50 Ω.
  2. Quarter-wave transformer: a section of line a quarter-wavelength long, of impedance $Z_T=\sqrt{Z_0Z_L}$. A 100 Ω load on a 50 Ω line needs a 70.7 Ω quarter-wave section. It works at one frequency (and near it).
  3. Lumped matching network: an inductor and a capacitor in an L-shape, which can match any real load to any real source at one frequency. If the load has reactance, you cancel it first (add the opposite reactance), then match the resistance. The antenna tuner in a ham radio shack is exactly this, with adjustable parts.
  4. Stub matching: a short open- or short-circuited line connected at the right spot adds the opposite reactance (a favourite on the Smith chart).

Try an L-match. Choose a 12 Ω load (typical of a short vertical antenna) and 50 Ω line at 7.1 MHz, and see the values and how narrow the match is as the transformation ratio rises.

What you now know

  • At radio frequencies a cable is a transmission line with characteristic impedance $Z_0=\sqrt{L'/C'}$ and velocity factor $<1$.
  • A load $Z_L\ne Z_0$ reflects: $\Gamma=(Z_L-Z_0)/(Z_L+Z_0)$, VSWR $=(1+|\Gamma|)/(1-|\Gamma|)$. 2:1 costs about 0.5 dB.
  • The Smith chart maps $Z_L$ to $\Gamma$; moving down the line rotates the point clockwise.
  • Cable loss rises with frequency: keep the line short at microwaves, or put the electronics at the antenna.
  • Match with a better antenna, a quarter-wave transformer, an L-network or a stub; use a balun for balanced antennas.
