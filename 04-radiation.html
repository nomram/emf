Waves leave the wire (and fade)

~30 min3 widgetsnear field, far field, 1/r, 1/r², 1/r³

We know a wave can travel. Now we need to start one, follow it out, and answer the practical question: how much is left when it gets there?

Launching a wave from a wire

Take a short straight wire and drive an alternating current $I\cos\omega t$ along it. Electrons slosh up and down (charges accelerate, chapter 1), so a ripple of $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ is shed every cycle. The ripple moves outward at $c$. By the time one cycle is done the first ripple is a distance $\lambda=c/f$ away, which is why wavelength and frequency are tied together.

The simplest antenna model is the Hertzian dipole: a wire of length $\ell\ll\lambda$ with the same current $I$ along its length. Its fields are known exactly. In the plane through the wire, $r$ from the centre and $\theta$ from the wire’s axis:

$$E_\theta=\frac{j\eta I\ell\,k\sin\theta}{4\pi r}\Big[1+\frac{1}{jkr}-\frac{1}{(kr)^2}\Big]e^{-jkr},\qquad k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$$

You don’t need to memorise it. Look at the bracket: three terms in $1/(kr)$. When $kr\gg1$ only the “1” survives and the field is $\propto1/r$. When $kr\ll1$ the last term wins and the field is $\propto1/r^3$. Between them, a mixture. The widget below draws exactly this.

Here $D$ is the largest dimension of the antenna. A 1 m dish at 10 GHz ($\lambda=3$ cm) has its far field beyond $2D^2/\lambda=2\cdot1/0.03\approx67$ m. A 25 m radio-telescope dish at 1.4 GHz needs $2\cdot625/0.21\approx6$ km. Why it matters: antenna ranges, RF exposure assessments and wireless charging all depend on which zone you’re in. NFC cards and wireless chargers work in the reactive near field on purpose: no energy leaves, so none is wasted.

How strength fades: the ladder of exponents

How fast a field falls with distance depends on the shape of the source. The question to ask is: how many directions can the field spread into?

  • A flat infinite sheet doesn’t let the field spread at all: constant ($r^0$). (Think of a huge capacitor.)
  • A long straight wire or line charge spreads sideways in a circle: the field is shared over a circumference $2\pi r$, so $E$ or $B\propto1/r$.
  • A point source (a charge, a tiny lamp) spreads over a sphere $4\pi r^2$: $\propto1/r^2$ for the field of a static charge.
  • A dipole (two opposite charges, a bar magnet, a small loop) mostly cancels itself from far away: $\propto1/r^3$.
  • A radiating wave is special. Its power spreads over a sphere ($\propto1/r^2$), and since $S\propto E^2$ the field falls as $1/r$, the slowest fade of any point source. This is why radio works at all.
Why do radio waves beat a static field?A static magnet’s field falls as $1/r^3$ and carries no energy outward. A radio wave’s field falls as $1/r$ and carries energy forever. At 100 m a typical bar magnet’s field has dropped to a millionth of its near value, while a radio wave’s is down to a hundredth.

Line and plane sources in radio

Real systems can be line- or plane-like too. A very tall vertical array of dipoles radiates like a cylinder: power spreads over $2\pi r\,h$, so power density falls only as $1/r$ (field $1/\sqrt r$) until the distance is about $h^2/\lambda$. A big flat panel behaves like a plane wave over a distance of $A/\lambda$ and then turns into a diverging beam. That is the origin of “pencil beams”: the near field of a large aperture is a tube of nearly constant width, which is why a radar dish can keep its power concentrated over tens of kilometres.

Spreading, power density and decibels

For an antenna radiating total power $P$ with gain $G$ (in the direction you look) the power flowing through each square metre at distance $r$ is

$$S=\frac{P\,G}{4\pi r^2}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad E_{rms}=\sqrt{S\,\eta_0}=\frac{\sqrt{30\,P\,G}}{r}$$

The second form is the handy one: $E=\sqrt{30PG}/r$ volts per metre for $P$ in watts and $r$ in metres. A 100 kW FM station with gain 4 at 10 km gives $E=\sqrt{30\cdot10^5\cdot4}/10^4=0.35$ V/m, a strong signal. A Wi-Fi router (0.1 W, gain 2) at 3 m gives $sqrt{30cdot0.1cdot2}/3=0.8$ V/m, a harmless level. What a receiver actually sees is received power, which is what the link budget in chapter 9 does.

Decibels in one minuteEngineers count in dB because ratios multiply and dB add. For power: $10\log_{10}(P_2/P_1)$. For field strength: $20\log_{10}(E_2/E_1)$. Double the distance: power −6 dB, field −6 dB (same thing, since $P\propto E^2$). Ten times the distance: −20 dB. A signal that has lost 120 dB has lost a factor of $10^{12}$ in power, which is routine for radio links.

Spreading is not the only loss

In free space the only loss is geometric spreading. In the real world waves are also absorbed (rain, oxygen at 60 GHz, trees), reflected (ground, buildings), diffracted around hills, and refracted by layers of air. Chapter 6 puts numbers on what different materials do at different frequencies. For now, hold on to the free-space law as the best-case baseline.

What you now know

  • An alternating current (accelerating charge) launches a wave that moves at $c$, with $\lambda=c/f$.
  • Near an antenna ($r<\lambda/2\pi$) fields are reactive and fall as $1/r^2$–$1/r^3$. Far away they become a true wave with $E,H\propto1/r$ and $E/H=377\ \Omega$.
  • The fade rate depends on source shape: sheet $r^0$, line $r^{-1}$, point charge $r^{-2}$, dipole $r^{-3}$, radiated field $r^{-1}$ (power $r^{-2}$).
  • $S=PG/4\pi r^2$ and $E=\sqrt{30PG}/r$. Doubling the distance costs 6 dB.
