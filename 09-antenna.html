Chapter 9 · Antennas

What is an antenna?

~40 min3 widgetsdipoles, patterns, gain, link budget

An antenna is a transducer: it converts the guided electrical energy in a wire into a free wave in space (transmitting), and does the reverse when it receives. Everything in chapters 1–4 is now applied: a current that accelerates charges makes a wave. The rest is engineering: how well, in which direction, at what frequency.

One antenna, two jobs: reciprocity

A wonderful result: an antenna has the same pattern, gain and impedance whether it is transmitting or receiving (for ordinary materials). This is the reciprocity theorem. It means we can study a transmitting antenna and know everything about the same antenna as a receiver. A good transmitting antenna for a given direction is equally good at catching waves from that direction.

The half-wave dipole

The classic antenna: a straight wire cut to about half a wavelength, fed at the centre. Why half a wavelength? Current has to be zero at the open ends of the wire, so the current wave bounces back and forth and forms a standing wave with a node at each end. A wire a half wavelength long supports exactly one half-cycle of that standing wave, with the maximum current in the middle where we feed it. This is a resonance: the reactance at the feed point cancels and the antenna takes power efficiently. The half-wave dipole gives $R_{in}\approx73\ \Omega$, a directivity of 1.64 (2.15 dBi) and a doughnut-shaped pattern: strongest broadside, zero along the wire. Rule of thumb for building oneLength in metres $\approx 143/f_{\text{MHz}}$ for a real wire half-wave dipole. (The free-space value would be 150/f; the factor 0.95 corrects for the wire’s thickness, an “end effect”.) For FM at 100 MHz: 1.43 m total, 71 cm per arm. This is why FM “rabbit ear” antennas look the way they do.

The monopole: a dipole with a mirror

Put one quarter-wave rod vertically above a good conducting plane (a car roof, the ground, a radial wire mesh). The plane acts as a mirror: the “image” of the rod below it is the other half of the dipole. So a quarter-wave monopole radiates only into the upper half-space, with twice the directivity of the dipole (3.28 or 5.15 dBi), has 36.5 Ω input resistance, and half the length. Whip antennas, AM broadcast masts and cell towers’ whips are monopoles.

Radiation patterns

An antenna does not radiate equally in all directions. The radiation pattern is a plot of the far-field strength against direction. You will see it drawn two ways: a polar plot (like a compass) and a rectangular plot with decibels. The vocabulary:

  • Main lobe, the direction of maximum radiation; sidelobes, smaller unwanted lobes; back lobe, the one behind; nulls, directions of zero field.
  • Half-power beamwidth (HPBW), the angle between the −3 dB points on either side of the peak.
  • Front-to-back ratio, main lobe divided by the back lobe, in dB.
  • E-plane and H-plane, the two principal cuts: the plane containing $\vec E$, and the plane containing $\vec H$.

Directivity, gain and effective area

Power can only go somewhere. If an antenna squeezes its radiation into a narrow beam, the power in that direction goes up as much as it goes down elsewhere. We measure that with three related ideas:

$$D=\frac{4\pi\,U_{\max}}{P_{\text{rad}}},\qquad G=\eta_{rad}\,D,\qquad A_e=\frac{\lambda^2}{4\pi}G$$
  • Directivity $D$: how much more intense the beam is than that of a hypothetical isotropic radiator spreading the same power equally in all directions. Isotropic: $D=1$ (0 dBi). Half-wave dipole: 2.15 dBi.
  • Gain $G$: directivity times the antenna’s efficiency $\eta_{rad}$ (power lost as heat in the metal or dielectric counts against it). Usually quoted in dBi (relative to isotropic) or dBd (relative to a dipole; 0 dBd = 2.15 dBi).
  • Effective area $A_e$: how much of an incoming wave’s power front the antenna “catches”: received power = (power density) × $A_e$. Notice $A_e\propto\lambda^2G$: at a given gain, a lower frequency antenna catches more. A dipole has $A_e=0.13\lambda^2$, which at 100 MHz is 1.2 m², much bigger than its cross-section.

Gain and area are two ways of saying the same thing. For a dish, $A_e=\eta A_{phys}$ with $\eta\approx0.55–0.7$, so $G=\eta(\pi D/\lambda)^2$.

What gain does not meanAn antenna does not create power. A 10 dBi antenna fed 1 W does not radiate 10 W; it radiates 1 W (less losses) but concentrates it so that in the main-beam direction it looks like a 10 W isotropic source. The product $P_tG_t$ is called the EIRP (effective isotropic radiated power) and is what regulators limit.

Polarisation

The polarisation of a wave is the direction of its $\vec E$ (chapter 3). Vertical dipoles send and receive vertical waves; if the receiving antenna is turned 90° it hears almost nothing (polarisation loss: $\cos^2\psi$, a 20–30 dB loss at 90°). Hence mobile radio uses vertical polarisation, TV broadcasts often horizontal; GPS and satellites use circular polarisation (rotating $\vec E$) so that orientation does not matter, at a fixed cost of 3 dB when mixed with a linear antenna.

Will the signal be heard? The link budget

Put it together. A transmitter of power $P_t$ with antenna gain $G_t$ sends a power density $S=P_tG_t/4\pi d^2$ (chapter 4). The receiver’s antenna collects $A_e=G_r\lambda^2/4\pi$ of it. Multiply:

$$P_r=P_tG_tG_r\left(\frac{\lambda}{4\pi d}\right)^2$$

That is the Friis transmission equation. The bracket is “free-space path loss” (FSPL, a ratio below 1), which looks like it depends on frequency, but only because of the receive aperture’s dependence on $\lambda$: if you fix the antenna areas instead of gains, higher frequency gives less loss. (This is why satellite dishes at 12 GHz are small yet have high gain.) In decibels, $P_r=P_t+G_t+G_r-\text{FSPL}$, which is how every link is designed. Three more numbers every antenna has

  • Input impedance $Z_{in}=R+jX$: what the feed line “sees”. We want it real and equal to the line’s impedance (50 Ω or 75 Ω): chapter 10.
  • Bandwidth: the range of frequencies over which the impedance and pattern stay acceptable. A thin wire dipole is narrow (a few per cent); a fat “biconical” is much wider; the log-periodic design is ten-to-one. Small antennas are always narrow-band: the Chu limit says that for an antenna fitting in a sphere of radius $a$ the product of bandwidth and efficiency is limited by $(ka)^3$.
  • Efficiency $\eta_{rad}=R_{rad}/(R_{rad}+R_{loss})$: the ELF antenna of chapter 8 taught us this one the hard way.

What you now know

  • An antenna converts guided waves to free waves and back; it behaves identically both ways (reciprocity).
  • A half-wave dipole: 73 Ω, 2.15 dBi, $L\approx143/f_\text{MHz}$ m. A quarter-wave monopole on a ground plane: 36.5 Ω, 5.15 dBi.
  • Aperture size in wavelengths sets the beamwidth; directivity $\approx4\pi A_e/\lambda^2$.
  • Gain is concentration, not creation. $G=\eta D$, $A_e=G\lambda^2/4\pi$, EIRP $=P_tG_t$.
  • Friis: $P_r=P_tG_tG_r(\lambda/4\pi d)^2$. Margin over the noise floor $kTB$ decides success.
