Doppler, PRF & FMCW

~40 min3 widgetsspeed from frequency, and the radar in your car

An ambulance siren drops in pitch as it passes. The wave’s frequency changes because the source is moving. A radar echo does the same, and measuring that shift tells you the speed of every target at once, which is how radars ignore mountains and find aircraft.

The Doppler shift

If a target moves toward the radar, each successive wave crest has a shorter trip, so the crests arrive closer together: the echo’s frequency is higher. Moving away, lower. For radar the shift is twice as large as for a one-way source, because the wave is Doppler-shifted once on arrival at the moving target and again when the target re-radiates it:

$$f_d=\frac{2v_r}{\lambda}=\frac{2v_rf}{c},\qquad v_r=v\cos\theta$$

$v_r$ is the radial component, along the line of sight. A target crossing perpendicular to the line of sight has $v_r=0$ and no Doppler. At 10 GHz ($\lambda=3$ cm) a jet at 250 m/s gives $f_d=16.7$ kHz: in the audio range, which is why early Doppler radars simply fed it into headphones. One full cycle of the beat corresponds to the target moving $\lambda/2$.

What Doppler gives you

  • Speed measurement: police speed guns (24 GHz K-band or 35 GHz Ka), baseball radar guns, sports and industrial sensors. A cosine error means the gun always reads a little low if not aimed straight along the road.
  • Clutter rejection (MTI and pulse-Doppler): mountains, buildings and the sea have little or no Doppler; aircraft do. Filtering out zero-Doppler echoes (moving target indication) removes most clutter, making a 1 m² aircraft visible over a 10 000 m² hillside, improving sub-clutter visibility by 40–60 dB.
  • Weather radar: the Doppler of raindrops measures wind inside storms; the “hook echo” and rotation signature of a tornado show up as adjacent inbound and outbound velocities.
  • Micro-Doppler: the wobbles of a helicopter’s blades, a person’s swinging arms or a drone’s propellers add characteristic sidebands around the main Doppler line, like a fingerprint. Used to classify targets and to tell a drone from a bird.
  • Medical and sleep monitors: 60 GHz sensors read breathing and heartbeat from chest motion of a millimetre.

Pulse radar and the PRF dilemma

A pulsed radar samples the echo once per pulse, so it sees the Doppler only through those samples. By the sampling theorem (Nyquist) it can recover shifts up to $\pm\text{PRF}/2$, hence an unambiguous speed of $\pm\lambda\,\text{PRF}/4$. Faster targets “wrap around” and look slower, or even stationary: these are the blind speeds $v_n=n\lambda\text{PRF}/2$. But raising the PRF lowers the unambiguous range $c/2\text{PRF}$ (chapter 12). Hence the dilemma:

$$R_u\cdot v_u=\frac{c}{2\,\text{PRF}}\cdot\frac{\lambda\,\text{PRF}}{4}=\frac{c\,\lambda}{8}\quad\text{(independent of PRF)}$$

You trade range against speed, but the product is fixed by wavelength alone. At X-band ($\lambda=3$ cm) $R_uv_u=1.1\times10^6$ m²/s: seeing 100 km without ambiguity limits speed to 11 m/s. Radars solve it by low PRF (long range, speed ambiguous: air-traffic control), high PRF (speed clear, range ambiguous: fighter radar looking for closing targets) or medium PRF with several staggered PRFs per dwell, and a bit of number theory to untangle the ambiguities.

Continuous-wave (CW) radar

You do not need pulses to measure speed. Transmit a steady carrier and listen on a second antenna at the same time; mix the echo with the transmission and the beat is the Doppler tone. This is the cheapest radar: the door opener, the burglar alarm, the speed gun. But a pure CW radar cannot measure range (nothing marks where in time the echo came from), and the receiver sees all the transmitter leakage.

FMCW: putting range back on a CW radar

The fix is to put a mark on the carrier: change its frequency steadily, a chirp. An echo from a target at range $R$ returns a time $\tau=2R/c$ later, when the transmitter has moved on in frequency by $S\tau$ ($S$ = sweep rate). Mixing the echo and the transmission gives a constant beat frequency

$$f_b=S\tau=\frac{2RS}{c}=\frac{2RB}{cT_c},\qquad \Delta R=\frac{c}{2B},\qquad R_{max}=\frac{c\,f_s}{4S}$$

Distance becomes a frequency, which a cheap FFT in a small chip converts to a list of ranges. The resolution depends only on the bandwidth $B$: a 77 GHz automotive radar sweeping 4 GHz resolves objects 3.75 cm apart. Doppler shifts the beat slightly; stack many chirps and a second FFT across them gives speed, hence the range–Doppler map that car radars use to see pedestrians, cyclists and other cars through rain, fog and darkness, up to 250 m.

Why 77 GHz?Short wavelength (3.9 mm) means small antennas with narrow beams on a chip, large bandwidth is allowed (4 GHz around 77 GHz), and the Doppler shift is large for slow targets (1 m/s gives 513 Hz). Rain absorbs about 1–2 dB/km, trivial over 250 m. And arrays of 3–4 transmit and 4 receive antennas form a virtual array of 12–16 elements (chapter 19, MIMO) that gives angular resolution of a few degrees.

What you now know

  • Doppler shift $f_d=2v_r/\lambda$; zero for crossing targets; sign gives approach vs recession.
  • Pulse-Doppler and MTI filters reject stationary clutter: moving targets stand out even over hills.
  • Pulse radar: $R_u=c/2\text{PRF}$, $v_u=\pm\lambda\text{PRF}/4$, $R_uv_u=c\lambda/8$. Low, medium and high PRF modes trade range against speed.
  • CW radar measures speed only; FMCW adds range through a chirp: $f_b=2RS/c$, $\Delta R=c/2B$.
  • FMCW with 4 GHz of bandwidth at 77 GHz is the basis of automotive and many short-range radars.
