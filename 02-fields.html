Chapter 2 · Fields from scratch

E, D, H, B & the hand rules

~30 min4 widgetsthe vocabulary of the whole subject

Textbooks use four field letters and students rightly ask “why four?”. The short answer: two fields describe what charges and currents do, and two describe what we put in. Once that is clear, the rest is bookkeeping.

The electric field $\vec E$

A charge $q$ changes the space around it. Put a second charge $q_0$ nearby and it feels a force. We define the field as “force per unit test charge”: $\vec E=\vec F/q_0$, measured in volts per metre (V/m). For a single point charge:

$$E=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2},\qquad \varepsilon_0=8.854\times10^{-12}\ \mathrm{F/m}$$

$\varepsilon_0$ is the permittivity of free space: how easily a vacuum lets an electric field form. Again the $1/r^2$ of a point source. Where there is a voltage $V$ across a gap $d$ the field is roughly $E=V/d$. A 230 V mains plug pin spaced 1 cm from the next has about 23 kV/m between them, and air breaks down near 3 MV/m, so there is plenty of margin.

The magnetic field $\vec B$

Chapter 1 gave us $\vec B$ as “what a current makes”. It has a definition by its effect, too. A charge $q$ moving with velocity $\vec v$ through a field $\vec B$ feels:

$$\vec F=q\,(\vec E+\vec v\times\vec B)\qquad\text{(the Lorentz force)}$$

The cross product $\vec v\times\vec B$ has size $vB\sin\theta$ and points perpendicular to both. That is the right-hand rule: point your fingers along $\vec v$, curl them toward $\vec B$, and your thumb shows the force on a positive charge.

Because the force is always sideways to the motion it does no work: a magnetic field can bend a path but never change the speed. So what does a charge do in a uniform field? It goes round in a circle:

$$r=\frac{mv}{|q|B},\qquad f_c=\frac{|q|B}{2\pi m}$$

The cyclotron frequency $f_c$ doesn’t depend on speed. That’s the principle of the cyclotron accelerator, and of the magnetron in a microwave oven. For an electron $f_c\approx 28\ \text{GHz per tesla}$, so a magnet of about 0.09 T gives 2.45 GHz.

Force on a wire

A current is lots of moving charges, so a wire of length $L$ carrying $I$ in a field $\vec B$ feels $\vec F=I\,\vec L\times\vec B$. That is the whole of an electric motor: current in a magnetic field makes a force, the force makes torque. Two parallel wires with currents in the same direction attract (each sits in the other’s field), opposite currents repel. The definition of the ampere used to rest on exactly that.

Why four letters? $\vec D$ and $\vec H$

Put a block of glass in an electric field and its atoms stretch into tiny dipoles, which add their own field. Put iron in a magnetic field and its atomic loops line up, which adds a lot more. So engineers split the story in two:

ElectricMagnetic
“Total field” (what a test charge feels)$\vec E$ (V/m)$\vec B$ (tesla, T)
“Applied” field (what the free charges / wires set up)$\vec D$ (C/m²)$\vec H$ (A/m)
Material law, linear case$\vec D=\varepsilon\vec E=\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r\vec E$$\vec B=\mu\vec H=\mu_0\mu_r\vec H$
Vacuum$\vec D=\varepsilon_0\vec E$$\vec B=\mu_0\vec H$
General$\vec D=\varepsilon_0\vec E+\vec P$ ($\vec P$: polarisation)$\vec B=\mu_0(\vec H+\vec M)$ ($\vec M$: magnetisation)

In words: H is “how hard I am driving” (set by amp-turns: $\oint\vec H\cdot d\vec l=I_{\text{free}}$), and B is “what I got”. Water has $\varepsilon_r\approx80$ (so it stores a lot of electric field), glass about 6, air 1.0006. For magnetism $\mu_r\approx1$ for almost everything, except iron and its relatives, where $\mu_r$ can be thousands. Radio engineers mostly work with $\vec E$ and $\vec H$ in air, where $\vec B=\mu_0\vec H$ and $\vec D=\varepsilon_0\vec E$, so the pair is interchangeable up to a constant.

One number links E and H in a waveFor a wave in free space the ratio is fixed: $E/H=\eta_0=\sqrt{\mu_0/\varepsilon_0}\approx377\ \Omega$. That “impedance of free space” returns in chapter 4 and in every antenna. A field of 1 V/m in a wave means $H=1/377=2.65$ mA/m.

Coils and electromagnets

One loop is a weak magnet. Wind many turns into a coil and the fields of all the loops add inside. For a long coil: $B=\mu_0\mu_r NI/L$ where $N/L$ is the number of turns per metre. Slide in an iron core ($\mu_r\sim$ thousands) and the field jumps by that factor, which is why electromagnets, relays, speakers and transformers all have iron or ferrite inside.

Magnetic materials, saturation and memory

The linear law $B=\mu H$ is a good start but not the whole truth for iron. Push $H$ up and $B$ eventually saturates: every atomic magnet is already lined up, so there is nothing more to gain. Reduce $H$ to zero and some magnetisation stays behind (remanence $B_r$), which is what makes a permanent magnet. You need a reverse field $H_c$ (the coercivity) to wipe it out. Run $H$ up and down and you trace a loop whose area is the heat lost per cycle. Choose materials below and compare a transformer steel with a permanent magnet.

Why this matters for radioAn antenna rod of ferrite ($\mu_r$ in the hundreds) concentrates the radio wave’s magnetic field into a coil, so an AM pocket radio can use a small coil instead of a 100 m wire. Ferrite is chosen because its loop is narrow (low loss) even at megahertz.

Fields hold energy

Every bit of space with a field in it stores energy. Per cubic metre:

$$u_E=\tfrac12\varepsilon E^2,\qquad u_B=\tfrac12\,\frac{B^2}{\mu}=\tfrac12 \mu H^2$$

In a travelling wave both are equal. For a 1 V/m wave: $u_E=\tfrac12(8.85\times10^{-12})(1)=4.4$ pJ/m³ and the same again in $B$. That tiny density, carried at the speed of light, is enough for a radio: $S=cu=3\times10^8\cdot8.8\times10^{-12}=2.65$ mW/m².

What you now know

  • $\vec E$ (V/m) is force per unit charge; $\vec B$ (T) acts on moving charge via $\vec F=q\vec v\times\vec B$, always sideways.
  • $\vec D$ and $\vec H$ are the “applied” fields; $\vec D=\varepsilon\vec E$ and $\vec B=\mu\vec H$ in simple materials.
  • A charge in a uniform $\vec B$ circles at $f_c=|q|B/2\pi m$; add $\vec E$ and the circle drifts at $E/B$.
  • Coils and cores multiply $\vec B$; iron saturates and has memory (hysteresis).
  • In free space a wave has $E/H=377\ \Omega$.
