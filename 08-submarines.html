Talking to submarines

~30 min2 widgetsthe cleverest corner of low-frequency radio

A ballistic-missile submarine hides because the ocean is opaque to almost everything: light, radar, GPS, Wi-Fi. But a submarine that never hears from home cannot receive its orders. The solution is some of the longest-wavelength radio ever used, with antennas tens of kilometres long and a data rate that would embarrass a telegraph. Everything you learned in chapters 3–7 comes together here.

The problem: seawater is a conductor

Seawater contains about 3.5 % dissolved salt, which splits into ions that move freely, giving a conductivity of $\sigma\approx4$ S/m (a thousand million times more than dry soil). Chapter 6 showed what that does: the wave is attenuated to $1/e$ in the skin depth

$$\delta=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi f\mu_0\sigma}}\approx\frac{250}{\sqrt f}\ \text{m}\qquad(f\text{ in Hz})$$

Every extra skin depth costs 8.7 dB. A submarine 10 m down needs a signal that arrives at the surface $10^{n}$ times stronger than the receiver’s noise, with $n=(10/\delta)\times0.87$. At 76 Hz the sea is a modest obstacle; at 24 kHz it is a serious one; at Wi-Fi frequencies the signal is gone before it has gone a hand’s width. Lower frequency, deeper reach. That is the entire strategy.

The table under the widget gives the reach for any budget you set. Two cautions: light and sound budgets are very different from radio ones (a laser link typically has 30–60 dB to spend, not 100, and an acoustic link also loses signal by spreading, which this table ignores). And look at the last column: the deepest signals carry the least data.

The price: very little data

A carrier at 76 Hz can only change by a tiny amount per second. Information theory (Shannon) says the maximum data rate is

$$C=B\log_2\!\left(1+\frac{S}{N}\right)$$

with $B$ the bandwidth. A channel can only use a fraction of its carrier frequency, say 1–10 %: at 76 Hz that is 1–8 Hz. At 24 kHz, 240–2400 Hz. At 100 MHz, 1–10 MHz. And the signal-to-noise ratio is low because the receiver is far below the surface and natural noise (lightning around the world, which is strongest in VLF/ELF) is large. Real figures:

  • ELF (76/82 Hz): on the order of one bit every few seconds, or a few characters per minute. The messages were short coded groups (“bell-ringer” signals) that tell the submarine to come up closer to the surface and listen to a faster channel.
  • VLF (15–30 kHz): 50–300 bit/s, enough for teletype-like orders. Reception requires the submarine to be within about 10–20 m of the surface, trailing a floating wire antenna or buoy, which limits stealth and speed.
  • HF/UHF/satellite: kilobits to megabits per second, but only when the antenna is out of the water.

Why the antennas are so enormous

An antenna radiates well when its size is a decent fraction of the wavelength. At 76 Hz, $\lambda=c/f=3945$ km, comparable to the width of the continental US. A half-wave dipole would be 1970 km long. So engineers build a short antenna and live with the consequences: $R_{rad}=20\pi^2(L/\lambda)^2$ is a fraction of an ohm, wire and ground losses in comparison are ohms, and nearly all input power is wasted as heat. The US Navy’s ELF system (Clam Lake, Wisconsin and Republic, Michigan, 1989–2004) was literally two tens-of-kilometres-long grounded cables running through forest, with megawatt-class power supplies; Russia’s ZEVS (82 Hz) near Murmansk is of a similar kind. VLF stations such as NAA in Cutler, Maine (24 kHz, about 1.8 MW) use huge “top-loaded umbrella” antennas hung from masts nearly 300 m tall, simply because it is easier to build wide than tall. Airborne VLF relays (the US E-6B “TACAMO” aircraft) trail an antenna wire several kilometres long behind a banking aircraft to make a vertical radiator.

Why can ELF/VLF go around the world?The space between the Earth’s conducting surface (ocean and ground) and the conducting ionosphere (60–90 km up) forms a waveguide for waves with wavelengths larger than the height. In it ELF attenuates at only about 1–2 dB per 1000 km. That is why a single transmitter in Wisconsin could be heard under the Pacific, and why the Earth–ionosphere cavity rings at its natural frequencies: the Schumann resonances. A perfect-cavity estimate of the lowest is $c/2\pi R_{\oplus}\cdot\sqrt2\approx10.6$ Hz; the measured value is 7.83 Hz, then 14, 20, 26 Hz, excited by about 50 lightning strokes per second worldwide.

The modern answer is therefore “layers”: ELF/VLF to say “come up”, then a high-speed channel when the submarine chooses to surface its antenna. Notice how this uses every idea in Part II: penetration (6), sound vs radio (7), photon and band gaps (5a), and antennas and bandwidth (9, 11).

What you now know

  • Seawater is a conductor: skin depth $\delta\approx250/\sqrt f$ m. Lower frequency → deeper reach, loss ≈ 8.7 dB per skin depth.
  • ELF (76–82 Hz) reaches tens to hundreds of metres, VLF (15–30 kHz) about 10–20 m; higher frequencies only reach the surface.
  • Bandwidth is a fraction of the carrier, so depth is traded for data rate: ELF is a few characters per minute.
  • Short antennas have $R_{rad}\propto(L/\lambda)^2$, so ELF/VLF stations are among the largest structures ever built and extremely inefficient.
  • The Earth–ionosphere waveguide lets ELF/VLF travel worldwide with low loss.
  • Laser, acoustic and satellite links complement the low-frequency “wake-up call”.
