Radar types & bands

~40 min2 widgetsa tour of the radar family

The same physics (a pulse out, an echo back, a time, a frequency shift) is built into radars that weigh raindrops, watch airliners, park cars, map glaciers from space and find buried pipes. What differs is the band, the waveform and the antenna. This chapter puts names on them.

The radar bands: L, S, C, X…

Radar engineers use a letter code from World War II secrecy that stuck. Each band is a compromise (chapter 12): a long wavelength gives long range through weather and with fewer problems, but needs big antennas and gives coarse angular detail; a short wavelength gives fine detail from a small antenna, but rain and gas absorb it.

Radar families

Primary and secondary radar

Primary radar uses the raw echo from the target: it works on anything, cooperative or not. Secondary surveillance radar (SSR) is a conversation: the ground station transmits an interrogation (1030 MHz) and the aircraft’s transponder replies (1090 MHz) with its identity (Mode A), altitude (Mode C) or full data (Mode S). The reply is stronger than any echo ($1/R^2$ rather than $1/R^4$), so SSR sees farther with little power. ADS-B has aircraft broadcast their GPS position spontaneously on the same 1090 MHz. Air-traffic control uses both: primary radar as the safety net for uncooperative targets.

By waveform

  • Pulsed (chapter 12): the classic, with high peak power; long range; coarse Doppler.
  • Pulse-Doppler: coherent pulse train; measures range and speed, rejects clutter. Airborne and air-defence radars.
  • CW and FMCW (chapter 13): low power, continuous; automotive, altimeters, level meters, short-range surveillance (“LPI”: low-probability-of-intercept).
  • Pulse compression, stepped frequency, noise radar: large bandwidth for resolution; noise radar uses random noise as the waveform for cover and jamming resistance.

By job

  • Search / surveillance: wide coverage, moderate accuracy: airport ASR, long-range 3D radars measuring range, azimuth and elevation.
  • Tracking: a narrow beam follows one target accurately. A monopulse antenna forms two or more overlapping beams simultaneously, and the ratio of their signals in a single pulse gives the angle error with no sensitivity to amplitude fluctuations. Track-while-scan follows many targets from successive sweeps.
  • Weather: measures rain by its reflectivity $Z$ (dBZ: 20 dBZ light rain, 50+ dBZ hail); Doppler winds; dual-polarisation separates rain, snow and hail by drop shape.
  • Marine: X-band (9.4 GHz) navigation, S-band for heavy rain; ship traffic and coastal surveillance.
  • Altimeters and terrain-following: aircraft radio altimeters at 4.2–4.4 GHz; satellite altimeters (Ku) measure ocean height to a centimetre.
  • Speed and traffic: police radar (K, Ka), roadside sensors, car sensors (24, 77 GHz).
  • Over-the-horizon (OTH): HF radar using skywave bounce (chapter 11a) or surface-wave guiding over the sea to see 1000–3000 km. Long wavelengths mean coarse resolution but strong immunity to shape-based stealth.
  • Ground-penetrating radar (GPR): low-GHz pulses into soil, ice or concrete (chapter 6): finds pipes, rebar, graves, bedrock, glacier layers.
  • Through-wall and life detection: UWB or CW radar that detects breathing behind rubble.
  • Planetary and space: radar astronomy (Goldstone, formerly Arecibo) maps asteroids and Venus; ground radars track space debris.

Radar imaging: SAR and ISAR

A radar can paint a picture. Synthetic-aperture radar uses the platform’s motion to synthesise a huge antenna. A 12 m satellite antenna looking from 700 km at C-band has a real beam of 3 km on the ground; SAR processing sharpens it to about 6 m. The result: all-weather, day-and-night images, used to watch floods, oil spills, forest, volcanoes, ships, and (by comparing phases between passes, interferometry) ground motion to millimetres. ISAR (inverse SAR) does the opposite: the radar is fixed and the target moves, for example a ship rolling in waves yields a 2D picture.

Radars with no moving parts

Many modern radars have no rotating antenna. A phased array (chapters 18–19) steers its beam electronically in microseconds, so one radar can search a sector, track tens or hundreds of targets, and pass guidance to a missile, all interleaved. An AESA (active electronically scanned array) has a small transmit/receive module behind every element, giving graceful degradation (a few dead modules barely matter), fast wideband beams, several simultaneous beams, and the ability to change frequency pulse to pulse, which matters for the resilience topics in chapter 15a.

What you now know

  • Radar bands trade antenna size, resolution and weather loss: VHF/UHF long range, S/C weather-proof, X fine detail, W compact sensors.
  • Primary radar needs only an echo; secondary radar (transponders, ADS-B) trades cooperation for range.
  • Waveforms: pulsed, pulse-Doppler, CW/FMCW, pulse-compressed.
  • Tracking uses monopulse; weather radars use reflectivity, Doppler and polarisation; GPR uses penetration; OTH uses skywave.
  • SAR synthesises a long aperture from motion: cross-range resolution ≈ D/2, independent of range.
  • Phased arrays steer electronically and multitask.
