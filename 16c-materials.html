Cage materials and the signal door

~45 min3 widgetsfrom conductivity to a working feed-through

Chapter 16a showed what a cage can and cannot do. Two practical questions are left. First: what should it be made of? Copper, aluminium, steel, mesh, fabric, paint, a metallised bag? Second, and harder: your equipment needs a signal to enter or leave. How do you pass a wanted signal through the wall while the outside interference is blocked, or cancelled?

Conductivity: the number behind everything

A material’s conductivity $\sigma$ (siemens per metre, S/m) says how freely charges move under an electric field. It runs from $6\times10^{7}$ S/m for silver and copper down to $10^{-16}$ S/m for plastics: more than twenty orders of magnitude, wider than any other everyday property. What the graph teaches
  • Metals are within a factor of 40 of each other (copper 58 MS/m to stainless 1.4 MS/m), yet at 1 MHz even stainless steel has a skin depth of only 0.4 mm: almost any metal sheet shields well once the frequency is high enough.
  • The gap between “metal” and everything else is huge: seawater is 10 million times poorer than copper, and concrete or soil another thousand times worse. Their shielding comes from absorption of a lossy medium, not from reflection.
  • Carbon fibre, graphite, conductive paints, polymers and ITO are in the middle (10⁴–10⁶ S/m): good enough for films and coatings, which is how they are used, but not for thick walls.
  • Magnetic metals (iron, steel, nickel, mu-metal) have a relative permeability $\mu_r\gg1$ at low frequency; in the skin depth it sits next to $\sigma$ and for low-frequency magnetic fields it can matter more than $\sigma$. This is why a steel box beats an aluminium one at 50 Hz and loses at 100 MHz.

So what should the cage be made of?

Total shielding is $SE=R+A+B$: the wave is reflected because the metal is a poor match to free space ($R$), the part that gets in is absorbed as currents heat the metal ($A=8.686\,t/\delta$), and multiple reflections inside thin sheets correct the result slightly ($B$, usually negligible). For a thin sheet, the film formula $SE=20\log_{10}(1+188.5/R_s)$ with sheet resistance $R_s=1/\sigma t$ is the same thing in a more convenient form.

The choice then comes down to frequency, field type (a radio wave or a magnetic near-field of a transformer or motor) and practical things: weight, seams, corrosion, cost, and whether the cage must be light, flexible, transparent or see-through. Practical notes behind the numbers

  • Seams decide, not the sheet. A 1.5 mm aluminium box has a theoretical SE of well over 150 dB. A real box gets 30–80 dB because of seams, joints and holes (chapter 16a). Choose the material that gives the best seam: copper is solderable, steel weldable, aluminium needs conductive gaskets or finishes.
  • Galvanic corrosion. Two different metals in a damp joint form a battery: aluminium against copper or steel corrodes; stainless and galvanised steel in contact is a common pair; tin-plated or nickel-plated contacts reduce the problem. Joints that corrode become open seams.
  • Magnetic fields at low frequency. Thin foils or paint do nothing at 50 Hz; you need thick conductive walls (eddy currents) or permeable ones (steel; mu-metal for low fields). Mu-metal is annealed and is spoiled by bending or shock.
  • Fabric and foil. Conductive fabric is the practical choice for flexible tents, bags and clothing; household aluminium foil works if wrapped continuously with folded, overlapped seams, but tears and creases make it unreliable; a “Faraday bag” for a phone is judged by a test, not a label, since the closure matters.
  • Mesh versus solid. Mesh lets air and light through but its holes set a cut-off, roughly $c/2g$, and the crossings must be bonded. See the microwave-oven door (chapter 17).
  • Buildings. Reinforced concrete is a coarse cage of rebar: 10–30 dB at best at GHz. A purpose-built shielded room is welded steel or bolted panels with knife-edge doors.
  • Weight. Per kilogram, aluminium and copper foil give by far the most shielding; fabric and paint are best for large flexible or retrofit jobs.

The signal door: a wanted signal in, interference out

Whatever the wall is made of, equipment inside needs data and power. We have seen the power side in chapter 16a (power-entry). For signals, the aim is to let the wanted signal cross while interference is blocked or cancelled. There are four principles, each with a place in real shielded rooms and test labs:

  1. Bond, then carry. A coaxial cable carries the signal on its centre conductor, and the outside interference runs on the outside of the shield. If the shield is joined 360° to the wall at a bulkhead connector, the interference stays on the outside. What leaks is described by the transfer impedance $Z_t$: a pigtail of a few cm destroys it.
  2. Filter. A low-pass filter mounted in the wall lets slow signals through and kills fast ones. It works only if the wanted signal is much slower than the interference, and the filter’s own parasitics (lead inductance of a capacitor) limit it to tens of MHz unless a coaxial feedthrough capacitor is used.
  3. Cancel. A balanced pair (twisted wires, differential driver and receiver) carries the signal as the difference of two voltages. Interference arrives on both wires equally: common mode. The receiver subtracts one wire from the other, so the common part cancels, to a depth limited by how well matched the wires are (a 0.1 % imbalance gives 60 dB). A common-mode choke raises the impedance seen by the common current only. This is how audio, Ethernet and USB survive a noisy world. It also works against interference that falls in the signal’s own band, which no filter can do.
  4. Leave the metal out of it. A glass or plastic optical fibre carries light, not current, and so brings no conducting path. It passes through the wall in a metal tube below cutoff: a waveguide too narrow to carry radio waves, whose attenuation is about 32 dB per diameter of length (chapter 16a). The shielding of the wall is untouched. The price is a transceiver inside the box and a way to power it (batteries or filtered power).

Choose a method and adjust it. Check the wanted bandwidth, add the broadband impulse and watch where the interference ends up inside.

Putting it togetherA realistic shielded-room design uses all of them: power through a filtered panel (chapter 16a), data on fibre through a below-cutoff waveguide array, analogue signals on balanced shielded pairs with common-mode chokes, and RF coax on bonded bulkhead connectors. Every opening in the wall is a waveguide below cutoff or a filter; the door has knife-edge or finger-stock gaskets.

What about “cancelling” in the sense of anti-phase?

Subtracting two equal copies of a common noise is passive cancellation by symmetry. People also ask about active cancellation: generating an anti-phase copy of an interfering field. It works for narrow bands and low frequencies where the field is known and slowly varying: active magnetic compensation of 50 Hz mains fields near an electron microscope, for instance. At radio frequencies, where wavelength is short, the field varies across the room and the interference changes quickly and unpredictably, so active cancellation is rarely practical. Passive shielding and balanced circuits do the real work.

What you now know

  • Conductivity spans 20+ decades; metals are within ×40 of each other, and with skin depth this makes almost any metal a good shield at high frequency.
  • $SE=R+A$ for a sheet: reflection from the mismatch, absorption $8.686\,t/\delta$. Thin films work through their sheet resistance.
  • Choose by frequency, field type and practical seams, weight and corrosion: copper and aluminium for RF, steel for low-frequency magnetic, mu-metal for static magnetic, fabric and paint for flexibility.
  • Signals cross a shield through a bonded coax, a filter, a balanced pair (cancels the common-mode) or a fibre in a below-cutoff tube; do each right and the wall keeps its rating.
