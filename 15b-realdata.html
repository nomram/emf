Radar in the real world: public data

~35 min1 big data widgetonly open, unclassified numbers

So far we computed radar cross sections from formulas. Now let’s look at what has actually been measured and published, and combine it with the public parameters of real radars to ask: how far could this radar see that target? Everything on this page comes from textbooks, standards, agency technical documents and open papers. Military radar performance and stealth RCS values are classified, and anything quoted in the media about them is flagged as unverified.

Where the numbers come from

  • The classic table. Merrill Skolnik’s Introduction to Radar Systems contains a table of typical microwave-band RCS values that has been reproduced in countless university lecture notes (it is the one in MIT’s radar course material and on many open sites): insect $10^{-5}$ m², bird $10^{-2}$, man 1, bicycle 2, small single-engine aircraft 1, small fighter 2, large fighter 6, medium airliner 20, large airliner 40, jumbo jet 100, automobile 100, pickup truck 200, small open boat 0.02, small pleasure boat 2, cabin cruiser 10, conventional winged missile 0.5.
  • Automotive radar literature (77 GHz): pedestrians −8 to 0 dBsm, cyclists about 0–7 dBsm, cars 10–20 dBsm, trucks 20–30 dBsm.
  • Drone detection papers (X-band measurements): consumer quadcopters about 0.01–0.03 m² (−20 to −15 dBsm), with rotor blades adding characteristic micro-Doppler.
  • Bird radar studies: small birds $10^{-3}$–$10^{-2}$ m². Flocks, insect swarms and rain add up into “angels” and weather echoes.
  • Space surveillance: public descriptions say that ground radars in the US Space Surveillance Network track objects down to about 10 cm in low orbit; smaller debris is detected only by special radars.
  • Stealth aircraft: the figures quoted in magazines and encyclopaedias (for example, about 0.025 m² for the F-117, about 0.1 m² for the B-2, and “marble to golf ball” for modern fighters) are claims or estimates, not published measurements. The real values depend on frequency, direction and condition, and are not public.
How to read an RCS numberAn RCS in a table is a single number from a whole pattern. It is usually the value seen at microwave frequencies and from a typical direction. Ranges of 20 dB are normal for the same object: a car seen from behind is much larger than from the front, and a bird flapping its wings varies from instant to instant.

Things the table teaches
  • A weather radar with 750 kW and a 45 dBi antenna sees a small bird or drone at a few tens of kilometres, but only because it is so powerful; an automotive radar is a different class entirely.
  • Range goes as RCS1/4: a 10 000× difference in RCS between a jumbo jet and a bird means only a 10× difference in detection range.
  • For a small X-band radar the main cause of missed targets is clutter (ground, sea, rain), not noise: that is why Doppler filtering (chapter 13) matters.
  • Frequencies matter: the Rayleigh scaling of chapter 15 means insects and raindrops are seen better by short-wavelength radars; large, resonant targets by long-wavelength radars.

Rain, snow and the weather radar equation

Rain is a cloud of billions of small spheres. For a volume of drops much smaller than the wavelength (Rayleigh region, chapter 15), the radar sees the sum of their cross-sections per unit volume, $\eta=\tfrac{\pi^5}{\lambda^4}|K|^2Z$, with $|K|^2\approx0.93$ for water and $Z=\sum D^6$ the reflectivity factor. Meteorologists quote $Z$ in dBZ: light rain is about 20 dBZ, heavy rain 40–50 dBZ, hail above 55–60 dBZ. A drop twice as big counts 64 times more: that is why hail stands out and drizzle is invisible. The same radar equation (with the volume of the pulse instead of a single target) is the “weather radar equation”.

What you now know

  • Open RCS tables (Skolnik and automotive and drone papers) give microwave typical values: insect $10^{-5}$ m² to jumbo jet 100 m², with a ±10 dB spread.
  • Stealth RCS values in the press are unverified claims.
  • Published parameters of weather, airport, marine and automotive radars let you compute detection ranges with the radar equation.
  • Range scales as $\sigma^{1/4}$; clutter, not noise, is usually the limit in practice.
