Space weather: solar flares, storms & the Van Allen belts

~45 min3 widgetsthe natural EMP

An EMP does not need a bomb. The Sun produces its own electromagnetic pulses (a flash of X-rays in minutes, a cloud of magnetised plasma in days) and Earth’s magnetic field captures energetic particles in two doughnut-shaped belts around the planet. This chapter links the natural “space weather” to the man-made pulses of chapter 16: same physics, different time scales.

One event can be R3, then S2, then G4 over a few days. Forecasts come from the NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center and from agencies and institutes in Europe (including SMHI and ESA’s space weather service), using satellites at the L1 point (a million miles upstream) that give about 15–60 minutes of warning of the actual solar-wind arrival, and the coronagraph images of a CME that give 1–3 days.

What it does to radio and navigation

  • HF blackouts (flares). X-rays raise the electron density in the D layer (chapter 11a), which absorbs shortwave. The effect is strongest at midday and fades with the flare (minutes to hours). VHF and above are essentially unaffected. Aircraft on oceanic routes that rely on HF have to use satellite links.
  • Ionospheric scintillation and total-electron-content changes (storms). GNSS signals cross the ionosphere (chapter 11a, $f_p$): storms make the electron content vary rapidly, causing errors of metres or even loss of lock; precision farming, surveying and aviation approaches are affected. Starlink and other low-orbit satellites feel increased atmospheric drag as storms heat and expand the upper atmosphere; in February 2022 a moderate storm caused the loss of dozens of newly launched Starlink satellites before they could raise their orbit.
  • Polar-cap absorption (proton storms) blocks HF routes over the poles; polar flights are rerouted.
  • Radar and radio astronomy see solar radio bursts as interference in the 10 cm–1 m range (a flare is a powerful radio source).

Geomagnetic storms and the power grid

When a CME arrives it compresses the magnetosphere and, if its magnetic field points southward, connects to Earth’s field and pours energy into it. Rings of current around the Earth and great currents in the aurora change the ground magnetic field by hundreds of nanotesla in minutes: this is the Dst index. By Faraday’s law (chapter 3) a changing magnetic field induces an electric field in the ground, and long conductors respond with geomagnetically induced currents (GIC): quasi-DC current in power lines, transformer neutrals and pipelines. It is exactly the physics of the slow E3 pulse of chapter 16.

Three well-documented events: the Carrington event of 1859 (telegraph systems failed worldwide); the March 1989 storm that collapsed the Hydro-Québec grid in about 90 seconds; and the October 2003 “Halloween” storms that caused a short blackout in Malmö in southern Sweden and damaged transformers in South Africa. The strong storm of May 2024 produced auroras at low latitudes and GNSS disturbances, but no widespread grid failure, a sign that operators and forecasting have improved. On 23 July 2012 a Carrington-class CME passed through Earth’s orbit and missed the planet by about nine days.

The Van Allen belts

Earth’s magnetic field is approximately a dipole. A charged particle in it does three things at once (chapter 2): it gyrates around the field line, bounces between mirror points near the poles where the field is stronger, and drifts around the Earth. Particles with suitable pitch angles stay trapped for months to years: the Van Allen radiation belts, discovered in 1958 by James Van Allen’s instruments on Explorer 1. Two main populations:

Orbits and radiation

  • Low orbit (ISS at ≈ 420 km, Starlink at ≈ 550 km) lies below the belts, except over the South Atlantic Anomaly, where Earth’s tilted, off-centre dipole is weak and the inner belt reaches down to the satellites: spacecraft there see single-event upsets and astronauts see flashes in their eyes.
  • Navigation satellites (GPS at 20 200 km altitude, Galileo at 23 200 km) orbit inside the outer belt, so they carry radiation-hardened electronics and shielding.
  • Geostationary satellites (35 786 km) sit near the outer edge and see electron storms; deep-dielectric charging has killed satellites.
  • Crewed missions to the Moon cross the belts quickly: the dose is modest, which is how Apollo astronauts passed them safely, though a long stay in the belts would not be.
When a bomb fills the belts

In 1958 (Operation Argus) and on 9 July 1962 (Starfish Prime, a 1.4 megaton explosion 400 km above the Pacific) nuclear detonations injected electrons into the belts. The 1962 artificial belt persisted for months to years and is said to have damaged or destroyed as many as a third of the satellites then in low orbit, among them Telstar 1, launched the day after the blast, which failed in 1963. It is also the event that produced the HEMP effects seen in Hawaii, 1400 km away (chapter 16). Natural belts can be as hazardous to satellites during storms, but a nuclear injection is different: it is sudden, hard to forecast and long-lived.

The practical consequence is an engineering one: power-grid hardening against GIC (neutral-blocking devices, transformer designs, operating procedures) addresses both natural storms and the slow component of HEMP; E1 protection (shielding, filtering, surge arresters, chapters 16 and 16a) addresses only the human-made fast pulse and ordinary lightning.

What you now know

  • Flares reach Earth at $c$ (8 min), energetic protons in minutes to hours, CMEs in 15–72 hours.
  • Flare class (A…X) and NOAA scales R, S and G summarise radio-blackout, radiation and geomagnetic effects.
  • Flare X-rays ionise the D layer and black out HF; storms perturb the ionosphere and GNSS and induce GIC in power lines: $I=EL/R$.
  • The Van Allen belts are particles trapped in Earth’s dipole field (gyration, bounce, drift): inner protons, outer electrons, with a slot between.
  • GNSS satellites orbit within the outer belt; the SAA brings the inner belt down to LEO.
  • Nuclear bursts can inject particles into the belts (Argus, Starfish Prime). Space weather resembles E3 but not E1.
