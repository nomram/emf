The spectrum

~30 min3 widgetsone wave, 25 decades

A 50 Hz mains wire, an FM station, Wi-Fi, the glow of a stove, the blue of the sky, a dentist’s X-ray and a gamma ray from a collapsing star are the same thing: an oscillating electric and magnetic field. They differ only in how fast it oscillates.

Three numbers for one wave

Every wave has three equivalent descriptions:

$$c=f\lambda,\qquad E_{\text{photon}}=hf\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \lambda[\text{m}]=\frac{299\,792\,458}{f[\text{Hz}]},\quad E[\text{eV}]=\frac{1.2398\times10^{-6}}{\lambda[\text{m}]}$$

Frequency $f$: how many times per second the field goes up and down. Wavelength $\lambda$: how far the wave travels during one oscillation, which is also the spacing between crests. Photon energy $E=hf$ (with $h=6.626\times10^{-34}$ J·s): at the finest scale the wave comes in packets, and the energy of one packet is proportional to the frequency.

Rule of thumb$\lambda\,[\text{m}]\approx300/f\,[\text{MHz}]$. FM at 100 MHz is 3 m. Wi-Fi at 2400 MHz is 12.5 cm. A 30 cm ruler is one wavelength at 1 GHz. Radio people memorise this one.

Photon energy: where the “radiation” worry begins

A molecule needs a few electron-volts (eV) to be torn apart, and about 10–25 eV to lose an electron. A photon delivers its energy all at once. So:

  • A Wi-Fi photon carries 10 µeV. Millions of times too little to break a bond. No amount of Wi-Fi power can ionise anything; the only effect is heating, if the power is high enough.
  • A visible-light photon (2–3 eV) can nudge electrons in a molecule: the basis of vision and photosynthesis.
  • UV photons (3–12 eV) break bonds, and DNA is vulnerable: sunburn.
  • X-ray and gamma photons (keV and up) knock electrons out of atoms: ionising radiation, which is dangerous even at low power.

That is why the safety rules in the radio range are about heating (power density, W/m²) and the rules at X-ray and above are about dose. The converter lets you check any number yourself, including what happens inside a material where the wavelength is shorter.

Hot things glow: the Planck curve

Warm matter is full of jiggling charges, and jiggling charges radiate (chapter 1). A perfect absorber and emitter (a “black body”) radiates a spectrum that depends on only one number, its temperature. Hotter means more power ($\propto T^4$) and a shorter peak wavelength ($\propto 1/T$). This is the radio-frequency noise that limits every receiver, the infrared that thermal cameras see, and the colour of the Sun.

Note one beautiful fact on the plot: the peak for your body is at about 9.4 µm, in the thermal infrared. And the cosmic microwave background left over from the Big Bang, at 2.7 K, peaks at 1 mm, in the microwave. Radio astronomers listen to the afterglow of the universe with the same kind of dish you will meet in chapter 11.

How each band travels

Frequency also sets how the wave gets around:

  • Ground wave (below ~3 MHz): follows the curve of the Earth. AM stations reach beyond the horizon, and VLF/LF reaches thousands of kilometres.
  • Sky wave (3–30 MHz): reflected back by charged layers of the ionosphere at 100–400 km height. The reason shortwave radio and over-the-horizon radar can reach the other side of the globe.
  • Line of sight (VHF and up): the wave goes straight and passes through the ionosphere. Range is set by antenna height: $d\approx3.57(\sqrt{h_1}+\sqrt{h_2})$ km for heights in metres (with the usual 4/3 Earth effective radius).
  • Above ~10 GHz: raindrops and gases begin to absorb, and at optical frequencies clouds block the path.

Chapter 6 attaches numbers to “passes through” and “is blocked by”.

What you now know

  • $c=f\lambda$; $\lambda[\text{m}]\approx300/f[\text{MHz}]$. Photon energy $E=hf$.
  • Radio, microwave, infrared, visible, UV, X-ray and gamma are one family, differing only in frequency.
  • Below the UV the photons are too weak to ionise, so the concern is heating; above it, dose.
  • Everything warm radiates a Planck curve peaking at $\lambda=2898\ \mu\text{m·K}/T$.
  • Different bands travel by ground wave, sky wave or line of sight.
