Photons, atoms & ionising radiation

~30 min2 widgetswhy a photon is absorbed or ignored

Chapter 5 said a photon’s energy is $E=hf$. Now we meet the other half of the story: what the atom does with it. The answer explains why glass is clear, why silicon is dark, why metals shine, why UV gives sunburn, and exactly what “ionising” means.

Electrons only have certain allowed energies

An electron bound to an atom is a wave trapped around the nucleus, like a guitar string held at both ends. A string can only vibrate at certain notes (the fundamental and its harmonics), and an electron wave can only exist at certain energies. Between them there is nothing: no allowed state. For hydrogen the allowed energies are

$$E_n=-\frac{13.6\ \text{eV}}{n^2},\qquad n=1,2,3,\dots$$

The minus sign means “bound”: zero is a free electron at rest far away. The ground state $n=1$ is at $-13.6$ eV, the next at $-3.4$ eV, then $-1.51$, $-0.85$ and so on, squeezing together toward zero.

About the picture: not the Bohr model all the wayYou may have seen electrons drawn as little planets on orbits (Bohr, 1913). That cartoon gets the energy levels exactly right for hydrogen, which is why we use it here, but real electrons are not on tracks. They are spread-out probability clouds called orbitals, and the levels in this guide are the energies of those clouds. The widget below therefore shows an energy ladder, the part that is physically solid, and a moving dot only as a visual aid.

A photon is absorbed only if it fits

A photon delivers its energy all at once, in one gulp, or not at all. So when a photon meets an atom there are three outcomes:

  1. Exactly the right size for a jump: $E_\text{photon}=E_\text{upper}-E_\text{lower}$. It is absorbed and the electron jumps up (excitation). A moment later (nanoseconds) it falls back and emits a photon, usually in a random direction.
  2. Not the right size (too small or between the allowed rungs): it passes through. The atom is transparent to it.
  3. Bigger than the binding energy ($>13.6$ eV for hydrogen): the electron is torn free with the surplus as kinetic energy $KE=E_\text{photon}-E_\text{bind}$. This is ionisation, and it is called the photoelectric effect. It is how Einstein showed in 1905 that light comes in quanta, and it earned him the Nobel prize.

Fire photons at hydrogen and then switch to a glass, silicon or metal slab. In a solid the levels of $10^{23}$ atoms merge into bands, with a forbidden gap $E_g$ between a full valence band and an empty conduction band.

Look at what this predicts and you can explain everyday things:

  • Glass is transparent to visible light because its gap (≈9 eV) is larger than any visible photon (1.8–3.1 eV). There is no jump for them to make. Photons of 9 eV and above (vacuum UV) are absorbed, so glass blocks UV-C and UV-B largely.
  • Silicon is dark grey because its gap is only 1.12 eV: every visible photon can lift an electron across, and that is exactly what a solar cell harvests. But infrared below 1.12 eV (wavelength above 1100 nm) passes right through, which is why silicon cameras and chips can be inspected with infrared.
  • Metals are shiny and opaque because the electrons are free to take any amount of energy and move together, so they absorb or re-radiate everything from radio to light.
  • Radio, Wi-Fi and microwave photons (≤0.001 eV) are far too small to excite any electron. They interact in different ways (making charges drift, rotating water molecules), and that is the subject of chapter 6.
Same idea, opposite direction: emissionRun the process in reverse and you get light sources. A neon sign emits only the specific jumps of neon (a few sharp colours). An LED emits photons of energy ≈ its gap when electrons and holes recombine. A laser stimulates many atoms to emit identical photons at once. Every “spectral line” in astronomy is an atomic fingerprint like those above.

What makes radiation “ionising”?

To ionise an atom or molecule you must remove an electron. That takes roughly 10–25 eV for most atoms of living matter (water 12.6, hydrogen 13.6, nitrogen 14.5, oxygen 13.6). Radiation with enough energy per quantum is called ionising: the far ultraviolet (UV-C and below ≈ 100 nm), X-rays, gamma rays, and also fast particles (alpha, beta, neutrons).

The ladder shows why the boundary matters so much. Ionisation creates fast, reactive fragments (free radicals) and can break DNA strands directly. Lower energies are not harmless (UV-C at 4.9 eV is above the energy of many chemical bonds, and photons of a few eV drive photochemistry), but only the highest ones ionise.

The most important sentence in this chapter

Photon energy, not power, decides whether radiation ionises. A 100 kW FM transmitter sends out photons of 4×10⁻⁷ eV each; none of them can break a bond, however many there are. A single 10 keV X-ray photon can ionise hundreds of atoms. So radio-frequency limits are about heating, and X-ray limits are about dose.

How X-rays and gamma rays interact

Three processes compete, depending on photon energy and the atomic number $Z$ of the target:

ProcessPhoton energyWhat happensWhy it matters
Photoelectric absorptionup to ≈ 100 keV (higher for heavy atoms)Photon is absorbed entirely; an inner electron is ejected. Probability $\propto Z^3/E^3$.Bones (calcium) absorb far more than soft tissue: that is the medical X-ray picture. Lead shields strongly.
Compton scattering≈ 100 keV – 10 MeVPhoton bounces off an electron, losing some energy and changing direction.Dominant in tissue at CT and radiotherapy energies; causes the fuzzy background in images.
Pair production> 1.022 MeVNear a nucleus the photon becomes an electron and a positron.Dominant for very high energy gamma rays. The 1.022 MeV is $2m_ec^2$.

A beam loses intensity exponentially as it goes through a material, the same law we will use for radio waves in chapter 6:

$$I=I_0\,e^{-\mu x},\qquad \text{half-value layer: } x_{1/2}=\frac{\ln2}{\mu}$$

$\mu$ is the attenuation coefficient. For 100 keV X-rays, lead has a half-value layer of about 0.1 mm, concrete about 2 cm and water about 4 cm. That is why dentists drape a lead apron on you, and why a nuclear shield is thick concrete.

Dose: how much is a lot?

Absorbed energy per kilogram is measured in grays (Gy = J/kg). The biological effect also depends on the radiation type and tissue; the sievert (Sv) weights for that. For X-rays and gamma rays $1\ \text{Gy}\approx1\ \text{Sv}$. These figures are approximate typical values:

SourceEffective dose
Eating a banana (potassium-40)≈ 0.1 µSv
Dental X-ray≈ 5 µSv
Chest X-ray≈ 0.1 mSv
Long-haul flight (cosmic rays)≈ 0.05 mSv
Natural background, per year (average)≈ 2.4 mSv
CT scan of the abdomen≈ 8 mSv
Occupational limit, per year (average over 5 years)20 mSv
Acute radiation sickness begins (short exposure)≈ 1 Sv
Roughly half would die without treatment≈ 4–5 Sv

Note the six orders of magnitude between a banana and a lethal dose. Also note that radio waves do not appear anywhere on this table: their risk is measured in W/m² and tissue heating, not in sieverts.

What you now know

  • Bound electrons have only certain allowed energies. A photon is absorbed only if it exactly matches a jump, or, above the binding energy, ionises.
  • In solids the levels merge into bands. A gap larger than the photon energy means transparency (glass for visible light); a gap smaller means absorption (silicon); no gap means metal.
  • Ionising radiation (≳10 eV per photon) is UV-C, X-ray and gamma. Photon energy, not power, decides.
  • X-rays and gamma interact by photoelectric absorption, Compton scattering and pair production; intensity falls as $e^{-\mu x}$.
  • Doses are measured in sieverts, and typical medical doses are a few mSv.
